# Blackbox Protocol: The World Before 2026
## An in-depth public history of global events and their consequences, 1962–2026

**Setting:** Blackbox Protocol (Core)
**Status:** Expanded draft. This document builds on `01-GLOBAL-EVENTS.md`, which it supplements and does not replace. It sits beside the full player-safe manuscript, `lore/core/BLACKBOX-PROTOCOL-ALTERNATE-HISTORY.md`, which remains the authoritative Core public history. Where the two differ, the manuscript wins and the difference should be logged. Anything marked **(proposed)** is new in this draft and stays editable until reconciled.
**Audience:** Player-safe. Everything here is knowable by an informed resident on **1 January 2026** from reporting, archives, court records, memorials and scholarship. Classified material belongs only in `BLACKBOX-PROTOCOL-GM-SECRET-HISTORY.md`.
**Fiction notice:** Real events are labelled **(real)** and are described as they happened, as context. Invented events are labelled **(fictional)**. The Blackbox Initiative, its successor programs and every fictional company, agency, incident and person in this document did not cause, stage, worsen or secretly direct any real catastrophe, war, attack, disease or political event. No real person named here is implicated in any fictional wrongdoing.
**Rules notice:** Setting history only. No mechanics are added or changed.

## How to read this history

### Two kinds of event

The Core world is Earth with an altered past. Its most consequential divergence is the 1991 activation of a classified continuity program, the Blackbox Initiative, built on research begun after 1962. Almost everything else that happened in the real world still happened. This document follows both strands at once:

- **Real events** are the great shocks of the last six decades: crises, wars, disasters, revolutions, crashes and pandemics. They happened. They were caused by the people and forces that caused them in our world. In the setting, they matter because of what they taught people and how institutions responded.
- **Fictional events** are the divergence: the research programs, contracts, exercises, accords, scandals, audits and hearings that make up the Blackbox legacy and the institutions around it. They sit alongside real history, usually as a *response* to it, never as its hidden cause.

The rule that ties them together is simple: **a real disaster is never merely a clue planted by a fictional villain.**

### The recurring argument

Read end to end, this history is one long argument between two reasonable positions. One says that modern life depends on systems so complex and interconnected that they need coordinated, sometimes secret, emergency planning to survive a crisis. The other says that any power exercised in secret, by people no one elected, will sooner or later be abused. Every fictional event in this document is a round in that argument. Neither side ever wins it outright, and both sides are full of decent people.

### What players can know

A resident of 2026 can know everything in this document, though most people know only fragments. Public knowledge has firm limits. People know there were classified continuity programs, that some contractors abused them, that courts and journalists exposed some of that abuse, and that the arrangements were never fully mapped. They do **not** know the full structure linking the 1991 Initiative to its successors, the classified annexes, or what any remaining program is doing today. Rumour fills that gap, and rumour is usually wrong.

## Part I — The Shadow of the Bomb, 1962–1990

### 1962 — The Cuban Missile Crisis (real)

For thirteen days in October 1962, the United States and the Soviet Union stood closer to nuclear war than at any other moment of the Cold War. Soviet missiles had been placed in Cuba; the US imposed a naval quarantine; leaders on both sides negotiated through slow, indirect and sometimes garbled channels while military forces stood at high alert. The crisis ended with a public Soviet withdrawal from Cuba and a quieter American undertaking regarding missiles in Turkey.

The lesson most widely drawn, in our world and the setting's, was about communication. Leaders with the power to destroy cities could not reliably tell what the other side intended, and messages took hours to cross. The direct hotline between Washington and Moscow followed in 1963.

**The divergence begins here.** In the setting, a small group of American and allied military planners, intelligence analysts, civil-defence officials and civilian systems engineers draws a second lesson. If governments can lose contact with one another in a crisis, they can lose contact with their own hospitals, power stations, railways and food distributors too. Their classified study of **continuity under simultaneous institutional failure** is designated **Project Looking Glass (fictional)**. President Kennedy and Premier Khrushchev remain exactly what history records: the leaders who brought the world back from the edge. Neither has any role in Looking Glass.

**Public consequences in the setting.** Civil-defence planning gains legitimacy across the Western alliance. School drills, fallout-shelter signage, emergency broadcast systems and government continuity plans spread, as they did in our world. So does distrust: secret planning invites suspicion, and the suspicion never entirely fades.

**Research threads.** Declassified planning papers from the 1960s, a retired civil-defence officer's memoir, and an old shelter inventory found in a county courthouse basement can all give characters a sense of how long the continuity argument has been running.

### 1963–1974 — Looking Glass and its critics (fictional, partly public)

Most of Looking Glass's work looks like ordinary emergency planning: redundant communications, government succession, secure records, transport priorities and relations with private infrastructure operators. Several recommendations pass quietly into normal law and administration.

What later becomes public is the existence of an argument inside the program. One faction, later called the **custodial school**, insists on strict civilian control, documented authority and automatic expiry of emergency powers. The other, the **continuity school**, argues that some essential functions must keep operating even when no lawful authority can be reached. Scholars of the period treat this split as the intellectual origin of everything that follows. Neither school is presented by serious historians as villainous.

### 1973 — The oil shock (real)

In October 1973, Arab oil-producing states imposed an embargo on several countries, including the United States and the Netherlands, in connection with the Yom Kippur War, and oil prices quadrupled within months. Queues formed at petrol stations. Governments introduced speed limits, daylight-saving changes and rationing schemes. Inflation and recession followed across much of the industrialised world.

**What the setting learned.** Fuel was not just a commodity; it was the thread holding together transport, agriculture, heating and medicine. Fictional continuity theorists push for strategic reserves and multiple supply routes, ideas that also took hold in our world through national petroleum reserves and international energy cooperation. In the setting, a sharper debate follows: if private companies hold emergency reserves under government contract, do they gain leverage over the governments that depend on them? Labour organisations and consumer advocates raise exactly that warning in public, and it becomes a standing theme.

**Ordinary life.** Older characters in 2026 may remember the queues, or remember their parents talking about them. The memory surfaces whenever fuel prices spike.

### 1975–1983 — The dormant architecture (fictional)

Budgets shrink and priorities shift. Looking Glass's central office closes. Some studies continue in compartmentalised defence, infrastructure and emergency-planning programs, and by the late 1970s a small circle of specialists has assembled a set of procedures known informally as the **Blackbox Architecture**: a paper framework for preserving critical information and coordinating emergency action across disconnected organisations. It is not a network. It has no operational existence. Public knowledge of it in 2026 comes from fragmentary later disclosures, and its most controversial idea, a last-resort continuity authority that could act when institutions had collapsed, is known only as a phrase in court filings.

### 1979 — The Iranian Revolution and the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan (real)

The 1979 revolution in Iran overthrew the monarchy and established the Islamic Republic, reshaping regional politics and triggering a second oil shock. In December of the same year, Soviet forces entered Afghanistan, beginning a war that lasted until 1989, displaced millions of Afghans and became a defining strain on the late Soviet state.

**What the setting learned.** These events changed alliances, migration and energy policy, and they were experienced very differently by Iranians, Afghans, Soviet conscripts, refugees in Pakistan and Iran, and people far away who simply saw prices rise. In the setting, as in our world, they belong to the people who lived them. **They are not part of any Blackbox scheme**, and a GM should resist any temptation to make them one.

### 1986 — Chernobyl (real)

In April 1986, a reactor at the Chernobyl nuclear power plant in Soviet Ukraine exploded during a safety test, releasing radioactive material across large parts of Europe. Soviet authorities were slow to acknowledge the scale of the disaster; the town of Pripyat was evacuated the following day, and radiation was first detected abroad by monitoring stations in Sweden. Firefighters, plant workers and the later "liquidators" who cleaned up the site paid an enormous price.

**What the setting learned.** Delay kills. The failure that haunted Chernobyl's aftermath was not only technical but informational: people downwind did not know what was happening. In the setting, the disaster sparks a long public debate about **independent verification**, the idea that emergency information should not depend on a single authority's willingness to share it. Fictional planners propose independent monitoring and notification networks. Critics point out the cost and warn that systems built to bypass a failing government could also bypass a functioning one.

### 1989–1990 — The fall of the Berlin Wall (real)

On 9 November 1989, East German authorities opened the border crossings in Berlin after weeks of mass protest. Within a year Germany was reunified, and across Central and Eastern Europe communist governments fell, mostly peacefully, to civic movements. Leaders such as Mikhail Gorbachev and Václav Havel are remembered for their real roles in that transformation.

**What the setting learned.** The pace of change astonished everyone. Western planners worried about nuclear stewardship, unemployed weapons scientists, broken supply chains and administrative vacuums. In the setting, a small planning group drafts a proposal to combine the dormant Blackbox Architecture with new international coordination mechanisms. At the end of 1990, it remains a proposal.

## Part II — The Divergence, 1991–2000

### 1991 — The Soviet dissolution (real) and the Blackbox Initiative (fictional)

The Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991, after a failed August coup in Moscow and declarations of independence by its republics. Fifteen successor states emerged. The transition brought hope, hardship, hyperinflation in places, conflict in others, and a long reshaping of Europe and Central Asia.

In the setting, concern about the speed of that change prompts a small coalition of officials from the United States and several allied governments to authorise the **Blackbox Initiative (fictional)**: a classified arrangement for emergency communications interoperability, preservation of essential records, logistics coordination, infrastructure continuity and narrowly scoped exchange of information about immediate systemic risks.

The two events are **contemporaneous, not causally identical**. The Initiative did not cause, shape or hasten the Soviet collapse. Historians in the setting date the true divergence of their world from ours to this year, because the Initiative created an unusual degree of cross-border, public-private operational integration under one classified framework. In 2026, the public knows the Initiative existed. The lasting public controversy is about who, if anyone, could authorise "exceptional coordination", and whether that authority ever existed lawfully.

### 1987–1988 — The Tanker War and Operation Earnest Will (real)

During the Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988), both sides attacked oil shipping in the Persian Gulf. From 1987 the United States reflagged Kuwaiti tankers and escorted them through the Gulf under Operation Earnest Will, the largest naval convoy operation since the Second World War.

**In the setting**, this is where **Cindrix (fictional)** first works in a conflict zone, maintaining communications equipment and providing contracted security and logistics at support facilities. The war and the escort operation happened as history records; no fictional company caused or directed them.

### 1990–1991 — The Gulf War (real)

In August 1990, Iraq invaded Kuwait. A US-led coalition assembled forces in Saudi Arabia under Operation Desert Shield, and in January and February 1991 Operation Desert Storm expelled Iraqi forces from Kuwait after an air campaign and a short ground war.

**In the setting** the war happens as history records. It is also the moment private contractors become a visible part of modern warfare's logistics. Among them is **Cindrix (fictional)**, a communications and electrical components supplier already established in the region since Earnest Will, which wins much larger contracts to supply and maintain communications equipment and to move and guard materiel with former service members hired as independent contractors. No fictional company or program caused or directed the war.

### 1992–1994 — The new contractor economy (fictional, set against real change)

The end of the Cold War released tens of thousands of engineers, analysts, logistics officers and security professionals into civilian markets in our world, and the setting is no different. New firms appear, established firms buy specialist divisions, and investors pour money into dual-use technology.

Three companies that dominate the 2026 economy begin their rise in these years, through ordinary business rather than as fronts for anything:

- **Cindrix (fictional)** grows through communications, systems integration and infrastructure services. It wins public praise for restoring communications during regional emergencies in the 1990s, a reputation it still trades on. Its slogan, *Everything. Within reach.*, comes much later, with its retail arm.
- **Veydran (fictional)** expands into biotechnology, advanced materials and medical systems. Its research partnerships produce real medical advances.
- **Vantrell Group (fictional)** builds a financing and acquisitions network. Some regulators scrutinise its methods from the start.

None of the three is intrinsically good or evil. Each is a reliable employer in one place and a troubling influence in another.

**Ordinary life.** Characters whose parents worked in defence or aerospace may remember lay-offs, relocations and the scramble for new careers. Many people of that generation ended up working for one of the new contractors without ever knowing what classified work, if any, their employer did.

### 1994 — The Rwandan genocide (real)

Between April and July 1994, around 800,000 people, mostly Tutsi along with moderate Hutu, were murdered in Rwanda. The international community, including UN peacekeepers on the ground, failed to stop the killing. The genocide is one of the defining moral failures of the late twentieth century.

**In the setting** the genocide remains exactly what it was: a crime committed by its perpetrators and a failure of political will by those who could have intervened. **It is not a fictional program operation and no Blackbox system played any part.** Scholars in the setting cite it in a specific debate: whether better coordination and communication could ever substitute for the willingness to act. Most conclude it could not. This history treats the subject with care, and so should any table that touches it.

### 1995 — The North Atlantic Relay Exercise (fictional)

A multinational communications exercise shows that civilian and government teams from several countries can restore limited essential connectivity after simulated failures. Published accounts praise its redundancy. They also record a dispute over who owned the maintenance records for the shared equipment, a seemingly minor quarrel that later reporting traces forward into the custody problems of the 2010s. Engineers who took part are now in their fifties and sixties; some will talk.

### 1996–1997 — The civilian boundary debate (fictional)

Legislators, inspectors general and civil-liberties groups in several countries raise concerns about secret procurement and the transfer of public responsibilities to private firms. They do not know about the Initiative, but they correctly identify real problems in programs adjacent to it. Some countries tighten review; others accept security arguments against disclosure. The resulting patchwork, strict oversight here and almost none there, becomes one of the defining features of the whole legacy.

### 1997 — The Asian financial crisis (real)

Beginning with the collapse of the Thai baht in July 1997, currency and banking crises spread through Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia, the Philippines and beyond. Economies contracted sharply, unemployment rose and, in Indonesia, the crisis contributed to the fall of the Suharto government in 1998.

**What the setting learned.** Money moves across borders faster than governments can respond. In the setting, fictional continuity contractors start offering transaction-continuity and critical-service contingency planning to banks and governments. Across Southeast Asia, the crisis is remembered less for contractors than for families who lost savings and businesses, and for the long recovery that followed. Filipino, Thai, Korean and Indonesian characters will each have their own family stories of these years.

### 1998 — The Port Meridian Contract Dispute (fictional)

A modernisation project at **Port Meridian (fictional)**, an international port, becomes a long public quarrel. Workers, rival bidders, local officials and journalists contest how the winning consortium got its advantage and why certain records were sealed. The port project itself is legitimate, and the new terminals still operate in 2026.

**What the public knows.** Several officials were removed from their posts. A junior analyst tried to raise concerns and was blocked. Some records remain sealed. The beneficiaries were not all held accountable. **What the public does not know** belongs to the GM. Port Meridian is the first incident many 2026 journalists reach for when they want to illustrate secrecy protecting itself.

### 1999–2000 — The millennium transition (real, with fictional participation)

The worldwide effort to fix the "Y2K" date problem in computer systems involved governments, utilities, banks, airlines and technology firms in years of remediation and testing. When the date turned, very little went wrong, a success that some later dismissed as proof the problem had been exaggerated.

**In the setting**, Blackbox-linked planners took part in some of this work through ordinary contracts that never mentioned the Initiative. Supporters of coordinated resilience planning point to Y2K as proof it works. Critics answer that the same success entrenched private operators in functions that needed stronger democratic oversight. Both arguments are still made in 2026.

## Part III — Security and Expansion, 2001–2010

### 2001 — The September 11 attacks (real)

On 11 September 2001, al-Qaeda terrorists hijacked four airliners, crashing two into the World Trade Center in New York and one into the Pentagon; the fourth crashed in a field in Pennsylvania after passengers fought back. Nearly 3,000 people were killed. The attacks transformed security policy worldwide and led to the war in Afghanistan and, later, profound changes in surveillance, aviation security and intelligence cooperation.

**In the setting** the attacks happened exactly as history records, and they were carried out by those who carried them out. **The Blackbox Initiative did not cause, permit or stage them**, and this history does not entertain claims that it did. Such claims exist in the setting, as conspiracy theories exist in ours, and they are false.

**What changed in the setting.** Demand surges for intelligence interoperability, emergency response and cross-agency coordination. Some Blackbox-derived systems prove useful, and officials seek to extend them well beyond the narrow continuity purposes of 1991. The legal objection, that tools appropriate to a catastrophe may not be appropriate to routine investigation or broad monitoring, becomes the central question of the next fifteen years.

### 2002–2003 — The security-contracting boom (real pattern, fictional detail)

In our world, the early 2000s saw rapid growth in private security, logistics and intelligence-support contracting alongside the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. The setting follows that pattern, with greater dependence on private systems for coordination and technical services. Oversight reviews find inconsistent standards for contractor access to sensitive information. Across the Middle East, governments respond in different ways: some partner with foreign firms on border and infrastructure security; others object to foreign contractors near sensitive domestic systems.

**Ordinary life.** A generation of veterans and contractors enters the 2026 workforce from these years. Some work in private security; some run small businesses; some carry injuries visible and invisible. Their experiences are diverse and should be treated as such.

### 2004 — The Indian Ocean tsunami (real) and the Meridian Accords (fictional)

On 26 December 2004, a magnitude 9.1 earthquake off Sumatra generated a tsunami that killed around 230,000 people across fourteen countries, with Indonesia's Aceh province, Sri Lanka, India and Thailand suffering most. The disaster prompted one of the largest humanitarian responses in history and led to the creation of an Indian Ocean tsunami warning system.

**What the setting learned.** Warning systems save lives only if warnings reach people, and recovery depends on local knowledge as much as outside aid. Fishing communities, local officials and religious organisations led much of the immediate response.

The same year, a limited group of governments signs the **Meridian Accords (fictional)**, setting standards for emergency information sharing and contractor-supported continuity coordination. The Accords are an independent institutional development of the period; they are **not** a response engineered by, or contingent on, the tsunami. They are not a world treaty and do not override national law. Several democracies decline to join or insist on parliamentary review, and regional powers outside the framework develop competing standards. Public knowledge in 2026 includes the Accords' published text and the fact that classified annexes exist. Their contents do not belong to players.

### 2005 — Hurricane Katrina (real)

In August 2005, Hurricane Katrina struck the US Gulf Coast. Levee failures flooded about 80 per cent of New Orleans. More than 1,300 people died, hundreds of thousands were displaced, and the slow, poorly coordinated government response became a national scandal and a lasting case study in emergency management.

**What the setting learned.** Evacuation plans that assume everyone has a car fail the people without one. Accountability matters as much as capability. In the setting, fictional professional associations of emergency managers and logisticians call for **auditable logistics chains**, so that anyone could later reconstruct who sent what, where and when. The demand becomes a recurring reform theme.

### 2005–2006 — From Initiative to Protocol (fictional)

As programs multiply, administrators adopt a common set of high-security procedures for access, emergency authority, record preservation and cross-system coordination. These procedures are formally designated **Blackbox Protocol**. At first the name applies only to those rules; over time insiders use it for the whole network of successor programs. Old hands still say *the Initiative*; technical staff say *the Protocol*; public documents usually say neither. Players who encounter the term in 2026 should know that its meaning depends on who is using it.

### 2007–2009 — The global financial crisis (real)

The collapse of the US housing bubble and of mortgage-backed securities triggered a banking crisis that peaked with the failure of Lehman Brothers in September 2008. Governments bailed out banks; millions lost homes and jobs; recession spread worldwide, followed in Europe by a sovereign-debt crisis.

**What the setting learned.** In the setting, emergency logistics and communications contracts stay stable while other sectors shrink, which accelerates consolidation. Well-capitalised firms, Vantrell Group foremost among them, buy smaller specialists along with their long-term government agreements. Some of those deals save jobs and services; others concentrate ownership and weaken competition. Public anger, much of it justified, grows at contractors who seem insulated from the risks ordinary households bear. Displaced workers, homeowners facing foreclosure and small businesses that failed are as central to this history as any executive.

### 2009–2010 — The accountability wave (fictional, set against real cyber incidents)

Legislative committees in several countries review contracting, data retention and emergency powers. Inspectors general uncover genuine waste and conflicts of interest, and real reforms follow. At the same time, real-world cyber incidents of the period show how vulnerable connected infrastructure has become. Security officials argue that dismantling coordination networks without replacements would make things worse. The decade ends on the contradiction that defines the setting: **the systems people distrust are the systems they increasingly depend on.**

## Part IV — The Age of Influence, 2011–2019

### 2011 — The Arab Spring and Fukushima (real)

From December 2010, mass protests spread across Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, Syria, Yemen, Bahrain and beyond. Outcomes varied enormously: a democratic transition in Tunisia, a revolution and later military takeover in Egypt, civil wars in Libya, Syria and Yemen. In March 2011, a magnitude 9.0 earthquake and tsunami struck northeastern Japan, killing nearly 20,000 people and causing meltdowns at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant, which forced mass evacuations.

**What the setting learned.** The Arab Spring showed how quickly networked communication could mobilise people, and how differently those movements unfolded in different countries; this history refuses any one-size-fits-all account of them, and so should a GM. Fukushima revived every debate Chernobyl had started about emergency communication, independent verification and public trust in official information. Neither event is connected to any fictional program.

### 2011 onward — Networked public life (real, with fictional contribution)

Smartphones, social media, cloud services and digital payments become central to daily life. The benefits are uneven between cities and rural areas, rich and poor. Blackbox-related contractors contribute to some public-facing technologies, but **the program is not the secret origin of the internet, smartphones or modern digital culture**. Its influence lies in particular continuity standards, contracts and institutional relationships.

### 2012 — The Cascadia Continuity Review (fictional)

A major earthquake-response exercise in the Pacific Northwest, built around a simulated Cascadia Subduction Zone event, tests public-private emergency planning. Contractor-supported logistics speed medical supplies to some places, but incompatible databases delay help to isolated communities. The public review recommends open interoperability standards and stronger local authority, and several reforms follow. Emergency managers across the setting still cite Cascadia as the clearest lesson of the era: sophisticated central systems help, but resilience depends on local capacity and accountability.

### 2013 — Surveillance and trust (real debate, fictional detail)

In our world, 2013 brought large-scale disclosures about government surveillance programs and a global debate about privacy and security. The setting has its fictional counterparts: journalists and legal advocates investigate contractor access to information collected for security and infrastructure purposes. Some allegations are substantiated; others are disproven. Public trust suffers because legitimate secrecy makes verification so hard.

### 2014–2015 — Corporate rivalry and regional realignment (fictional, set against real trends)

Cindrix expands into infrastructure and communications management. Veydran strengthens medical and materials research. Vantrell Group finances energy, logistics and technology projects. They compete hard for contracts, talent and influence. In Europe, governments debate privacy rules and reliance on foreign technology. In the Asia-Pacific, maritime tensions and trade routes draw attention. African and Latin American governments pursue their own infrastructure strategies, sometimes accepting foreign financing and sometimes building regional alternatives. A company's reputation depends on what it actually does in a given place.

### 2016 — The Harrowgate Affair (fictional)

A whistleblower gives journalists evidence that a contractor operating a Blackbox-related system monitored civilians beyond its lawful authority. Public hearings, court cases, independent reporting and official investigations follow. Some officials resign; several people are prosecuted; some accusations are dismissed and others substantiated. Reforms restrict access, require audit trails and create new independent reporting duties.

**What the public learned.** The contractor's misconduct, and the existence of several classified continuity arrangements. **What it did not learn** is how those arrangements connect to the 1991 Initiative. Harrowgate is the most famous fictional scandal of the Core era. Nearly every adult in 2026 has an opinion on it, and the whistleblower remains a hero to some and a traitor to others.

### 2017 — The Fragmentation Audit (fictional)

A classified multi-agency audit, whose headline finding later becomes public, concludes that **no single authority can produce a complete inventory of the systems descended from the original Initiative**. Some were retired, some absorbed into ordinary agencies, some transferred through corporate acquisitions. The audit explicitly rejects the idea that one hidden organisation commands the whole network. Its more unsettling conclusion is bureaucratic: no one has the full picture. Reformers want public accountability; technical staff fear hasty decommissioning will break services people rely on.

### 2018–2019 — The legitimacy debate (fictional, set against real trends)

Protests, elections, investigations, labour disputes and campaigns put institutional accountability at the centre of politics in several countries. Disinformation and genuine investigative reporting travel through the same channels, making it harder to tell documented wrongdoing from invention. Reform movements win contract cancellations and better disclosure rules. Some agencies and companies cooperate; others resist. The world is tense, but it is not uniformly getting worse.

## Part V — The Years of Strain, 2020–2025

### 2020 — The COVID-19 pandemic (real)

The COVID-19 pandemic killed millions of people worldwide, closed schools and workplaces, overwhelmed hospitals, disrupted supply chains and changed how people worked and lived. Vaccines were developed at unprecedented speed. Responses, and their results, varied greatly between countries.

**In the setting**, Blackbox-derived continuity systems help coordinate some medical supplies, communications and transport; others prove rigid and poorly suited to local conditions. Veydran and competing organisations contribute to medical research, and individual contracts face scrutiny. **There is no canonical claim that Blackbox, Veydran or anyone else in the setting engineered the disease or caused the pandemic**, and such claims are treated in-world as the misinformation they are. Personal loss is real in the setting, as it was in ours. Many characters will have lost someone, and the table should handle that with care.

**Local echo.** In west-central Texas, the community then called Heritage Ranch is better supplied than many neighbours during the shortages and sometimes helps them. Recruitment rises. Critics question whether members surrender too much independence; supporters point to real help. Neither view is settled in 2026.

### 2021 — Recovery and uneven dependence

Recovery varies sharply. Some communities see new investment; others struggle with debt, inflation, infrastructure backlogs and lost businesses. Cindrix promotes resilient communications; Veydran pursues medical and environmental research; Vantrell Group finances recovery projects. Inside each company, people argue about pricing, access, governance and political influence.

### 2022 — The Russian invasion of Ukraine (real) and the Strait Lantern Disruption (fictional)

In February 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The war killed and displaced enormous numbers of people, prompted sweeping sanctions and military aid, and reshaped European energy and security policy.

**In the setting** the war happens as history records. Fictional firms may respond commercially or through aid, but **no fictional organisation caused or directs it**. The setting does not reduce real wars to a Blackbox conspiracy.

Separately, the **Strait Lantern Disruption (fictional)** temporarily interrupts maritime communications along a busy Asia-Pacific shipping corridor. Investigators consider technical failure, criminal interference and state-sponsored action; the public record never settles on a cause. The Philippines, Japan and neighbouring states respond according to their own interests and domestic debates, and the event spurs investment in maritime safety and independent verification. For Filipino characters in particular, Strait Lantern is a live political memory: fishermen who lost navigation, coast guard officers who improvised, and arguments in Manila about who should be trusted with the region's communications.

### 2023 — The sovereignty question (fictional detail, real trends)

Several countries review foreign ownership of critical infrastructure and access to sensitive data. African governments and regional institutions negotiate tougher terms for energy, communications and mineral-processing investment. Latin American debates centre on ports, telecommunications, organised crime and local control of strategic industries. European procurement is shaped by data protection and industrial policy. No region is a mere battleground for outside powers; local leaders, unions, journalists and communities make consequential choices.

### 2024 — The Greybridge Hearings (fictional)

Parliamentary and congressional inquiries in several countries, grouped in international reporting as the **Greybridge Hearings**, examine emergency contracting, revolving-door appointments and improper access to public data. They find misconduct and effective oversight in roughly equal measure. Some companies lose contracts; others are cleared. The hearings do **not** reveal the Blackbox Protocol as a single coherent organisation, because it is not one. The open question they leave is the one 2026 politics is still arguing about: should governments reduce dependence on private continuity systems even if the transition temporarily raises risk, or reform them while keeping them running?

**Local echo.** The same year, a regional preparedness forum in Texas exposes disagreements inside Heritage Ranch over cooperation with outsiders, and in 2025 former members' complaints become a subject of local reporting. These are clues, not proof of anything.

### 2025 — The uncertain peace and the Orion Data Dispute (fictional)

The world enters 2025 with working institutions and unresolved tensions. International flashpoints remain active, economic competition sharpens and infrastructure security becomes a major public issue. Political coalitions break and reform. New civic groups appear alongside cynics who profit from distrust.

The **Orion Data Dispute (fictional)** begins when records from a defunct technology contractor surface in several countries at once. Some files concern ordinary procurement; others seem to reference obsolete Blackbox access procedures. Their provenance is incomplete, and forgeries circulate alongside authentic material. Journalists, government investigators, corporate compliance teams and criminal brokers all race to work out what is real. No one holds the full archive. Orion is the freshest mystery of the Core era and the most likely to draw characters in.

## Part VI — The World on 1 January 2026

### A world that still works

Ordinary life continues. Schools open, aircraft fly, ports operate, farmers plant, businesses hire, courts sit and governments govern. People build families, careers and communities. The Blackbox legacy is a set of contested institutions and half-understood archives, not a shadow government. **No future collapse is predetermined.** A Core campaign can run for years in a world that keeps working.

The events in this history are not just background. They live in the present in concrete ways:

### Regional lenses

The setting's initial detail favours the United States, but the world is global and every region has its own experience of these decades.

- **United States:** The primary campaign region. Small towns such as the approved Core starting town of **Briar Hollow, West Virginia**, and its unnamed county carry the memory of mine closures, the opioid crisis, the pandemic and the 2008 crash as much as any national event. Real places such as Clarksville, Tennessee, and Fort Campbell, Kentucky, may appear, with fictional people and incidents only.
- **Europe and the North Atlantic:** Strong oversight traditions made Europe home to some of the fiercest opposition to the Meridian Accords. Energy security and privacy dominate politics.
- **Russia, Eastern Europe and Central Asia:** The post-Soviet transition is lived history for anyone over forty. Experiences of the 1990s range from liberation to catastrophe.
- **East and Southeast Asia:** The 1997 crisis, the 2004 tsunami, Fukushima and Strait Lantern shape public memory. In the Philippines, local disaster networks and maritime politics are central.
- **South Asia:** The tsunami, rapid urban growth and digital-payment expansion shape the decades; local institutions drive outcomes.
- **Middle East and North Africa:** The oil shocks, 1979, the security-contracting era and the Arab Spring are experienced locally and diversely. No single narrative fits.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa:** Rwanda's memory, infrastructure-investment debates and the 2023 sovereignty negotiations. Local engineers, businesses and civic groups hold agency.
- **Latin America and the Caribbean:** Ports, telecommunications, organised crime and local control of strategic industries; financial crises with their own regional histories.
- **Oceania:** Island logistics, maritime communications and climate exposure.

These lenses are starting points. Future regional chapters need locally grounded authoring and research before publication.

## Research method for players and GMs

Every event in this history supports investigation. A good Core-era research thread has:

- **a dated event** with a location and the people and institutions involved;
- **at least two sources** that partly disagree, such as a court record and a memoir, or a news report and a union newsletter;
- **a plausible way to learn about it**: an archive, a witness, a memorial, a professional association, a leaked file;
- **a clear line between fact and inference.** A rumour is not evidence and not every mystery is a conspiracy.

GMs should keep the fiction firewall intact at the table: real tragedies are honoured as real, fictional scandals stay fictional, and no real person is accused of fictional crimes.
