# Global historical events and consequences
Player-safe historical expansion; real events are identified as real and fictional divergences as fictional. The Blackbox Initiative did not cause real catastrophes.

## 1962–1989
**1962 Cuban Missile Crisis (real).** Nuclear brinkmanship exposes fragile communications and the consequences of mistaken assumptions. Kennedy and Khrushchev remain historical figures, not fictional conspirators. Fictional Project Looking Glass researchers subsequently study distributed decision-making and preservation of civilian services. Public consequence: civil-defense planning gains legitimacy, though secrecy invites distrust.

**1973 oil shock (real).** Energy prices expose the dependence of transportation, hospitals, and agriculture on fuel. Fictional continuity theorists advocate fuel reserves and multiple supply routes. Labor organizations warn that privately held reserves can give contractors excessive leverage.

**1979 Iranian Revolution and Soviet intervention in Afghanistan (real).** Regional political changes and war reshape alliances, migration, and energy policy. Distinct communities experience these events differently; do not assign them to a Blackbox scheme.

**1986 Chernobyl (real).** Delayed communication and radiological danger illustrate failures of emergency notification. Fictional architects debate independent verification networks, their cost, and the danger of bypassing elected oversight.

**1989 Berlin Wall opens (real).** Changing security assumptions and the reorganization of institutions create both opportunity and uncertainty for the personnel who will later propose Blackbox activation.

## 1990–2000
**1991 Soviet dissolution and Blackbox Initiative activation (real and fictional respectively).** The two are contemporaneous, not causally identical. The fictional program begins with limited continuity agreements. A lasting controversy concerns who may authorize exceptional coordination.

**1994 Rwanda genocide (real).** Humanitarian and diplomatic failures remain historical facts, not fictional program operations. Academic and public debate considers when coordination cannot substitute for political will.

**1995 North Atlantic Relay Exercise (fictional).** A technical interoperability demonstration connects civilian and government communications teams. Published accounts praise redundancy but disagree over private ownership of maintenance records.

**1997 Asian financial crisis (real).** Banking and currency shocks reveal cross-border dependencies. Fictional continuity contractors begin offering transaction and critical-service contingency planning.

**1998 Port Meridian Contract Dispute (fictional).** Workers, contractors, officials, and journalists contest procurement access and record preservation. The public dispute continues for years; classified wrongdoing belongs in the GM history.

## 2001–2010
**2001 September 11 attacks (real).** Public security priorities change. The fictional Blackbox legacy expands through contracts but does not explain or cause the attacks.

**2004 Indian Ocean tsunami (real).** Humanitarian response and damaged infrastructure illustrate the need for local knowledge and coordination. The fictional Meridian Accords are an independent institutional development of the same period.

**2005 Hurricane Katrina (real).** Evacuation, infrastructure, and accountability become central to emergency-management debate. Fictional professional associations call for auditable logistics chains.

**2007–2009 financial crisis (real).** Budget pressure increases outsourcing while intensifying scrutiny of contractors. Displaced workers, homeowners, and small businesses are as important to this history as executives.

## 2011–2026
**2011 Arab Spring and Fukushima (real).** Diverse political movements and a major nuclear disaster affect distinct countries; avoid one-size-fits-all narratives. Emergency communications, sovereignty, and transparency remain recurring public issues.

**2012 Cascadia Continuity Review (fictional).** Pacific Northwest planners assess regional preparedness and the legal custody of infrastructure records.

**2016 Harrowgate Affair (fictional).** Reporting exposes unlawful surveillance; court processes and public oversight establish some wrongdoing while leaving other allegations disputed.

**2017 Fragmentation Audit (fictional).** Auditors identify inconsistent obligations and missing custody links. Reformers favor public accountability; technical staff fear that hasty decommissioning will damage legitimate services.

**2020 COVID-19 pandemic (real).** The pandemic affects health, labor, education, and supply chains. The fictional setting preserves the reality of personal loss and uneven institutional responses.

**2022 full-scale Russian invasion of Ukraine (real).** War affects energy, displacement, and diplomacy. Fictional firms may respond commercially or through aid, without being written as the cause.

**2024 Greybridge Hearings (fictional).** Testimony debates emergency-contract oversight and data retention; a public record provides conflicting but researchable accounts.

**2026 Core baseline.** The world remains functioning and politically diverse. Players may discover historical consequences, but no future collapse is predetermined.

## Research threads
Each event supports archives, witness interviews, policy debates, local memorials, professional disagreements, and conflicting sources. A real disaster is never merely a clue planted by a fictional villain.
