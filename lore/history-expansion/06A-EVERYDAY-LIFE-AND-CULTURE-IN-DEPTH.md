# Blackbox Protocol: How People Live
## An in-depth social history of everyday life and culture, Core baseline 1 January 2026

**Setting:** Blackbox Protocol (Core)
**Status:** Expanded draft. Supplements `06-EVERYDAY-LIFE-AND-CULTURE.md`; does not replace it. Items marked **(proposed)** are new and editable. Fictional media names still need collision checks before publication.
**Audience:** Player-safe.
**Fiction notice:** Real culture, technology and social trends appear as they are. No false real-world awards, scandals or events are invented. Heritage Ranch is a fictional survivalist compound, not a stand-in for any real denomination, community or Texas county.
**Rules notice:** Setting history only. Where this document touches skills, wealth, equipment or learning, the rules govern.

---

## How to use this history

### The point of ordinary life

The Core world is a recognisable present. Most people in it never touch a classified program, never testify at a hearing and never read a leaked file. They pay rent, raise children, care for parents, go to work, worry about prices and argue about politics. A continuity contractor's decisions can reach into their lives, through a delayed delivery, an outage or a changed contract, without turning those lives into a conspiracy plot.

This document describes that ordinary life in enough detail for characters to live in it. It also does one quiet job for the other two eras: it records what people in 2026 rely on, know and do not know. Those facts become the starting point for any character who later crosses into a harder world. Nothing here predicts that crossing.

### Three rules for the table

1. **The present works.** Schools, hospitals, shops, courts, roads and phones function, unevenly and with frustrations, but they function.
2. **Not everything is connected.** A records error is usually a records error. A rumour is not evidence.
3. **People differ.** Region, income, age, religion, disability, culture and family history shape every life described here.

## Part I — Home and family

### Households

Households in 2026 come in every shape: nuclear families, single parents, multigenerational homes, roommates, couples without children, people living alone, extended kin networks. Housing costs dominate budgets in cities; in rural areas, distance and transport costs bite instead. Many adults care for both children and ageing parents. Remote work, which spread during the pandemic, lets some people live far from their employers and leaves others in jobs that cannot be done from home.

### The weekly rhythm

A typical weekday involves commuting or logging on, school drop-offs, work, errands, meals and screens. Weekends bring sport, worship, family visits, chores and, for many, a second job. Deliveries arrive at the door. Groceries come from a supermarket, a local market or **Cindrix Fresh**. Prescriptions come from a pharmacy or **Cindrix Health**. Payments go through cards, apps and **Cindrix Pay**, though cash still matters for many small businesses, older people and anyone without reliable banking.

### Money and worry

The 2026 household worries about rent or mortgage payments, food prices, childcare, healthcare costs, debt, car repairs and retirement. Wealth is held mostly as bank balances, retirement accounts, home equity and wages, all of which depend on working institutions. Practical assets such as tools, gardens, workshops, livestock and trades are held by fewer people than a generation ago, and those people are often rural, older or working-class. That distribution matters to the rules on wealth in later eras, which is why it is recorded here.

### Life stages in 2026

- **Children and teenagers** have never known a world without smartphones. School, sport, friends and social media fill their lives.
- **Young adults** face high housing costs and changing job markets; many work in gig, retail, logistics or service jobs, often for Cindrix in some form.
- **Middle-aged adults** carry the heaviest load of work and care. Many remember the 2008 crash as the event that reset their careers.
- **Older adults** remember the Cold War, the oil shocks and a world before the internet. Some still keep paper records, cash at home and a garden, habits their grandchildren find quaint.

## Part II — Work and trade

### What people do

The Core economy runs on healthcare, teaching, retail, logistics, trucking, construction, maintenance, manufacturing, technology, public service, farming, ranching and the skilled trades. The occupation roster in the rules reflects that range. Most jobs depend on supply chains and digital systems that workers do not control.

### The contractor economy, seen from below

Since the 1990s, many public functions have been run by private contractors: utilities, emergency communications, IT, security, logistics. For a worker, that can mean good pay and training, or it can mean fixed-term contracts, confidentiality agreements and the sense that the real decisions happen somewhere else. Union archives from Port Meridian onward are full of workers asking what their contracts actually require of them.

### Trades and repair

The skilled trades are in demand and short of people. Electricians, line workers, plumbers, mechanics, welders and heavy-equipment operators are often older than average, and many towns depend on a handful of them. Repair culture survives in workshops, farm sheds, volunteer fire halls and online forums. A proposed volunteer publication, **Repair Commons**, collects maintenance tips and annotated manuals, and is popular with ORG-01 crews and hobbyists alike.

### Farming and ranching

Agriculture is highly productive and highly dependent on fuel, fertiliser, seed companies, machinery dealers and credit. Cattle ranching in Texas, grain and dairy in places like **Calder County**, and fishing on coasts like the **Aldane Coast** all run on long supply lines. Cooperatives such as **Caleb Voss**'s share equipment and labour. **Harvest & Homestead** supplies seeds and preservation equipment to small farms and hobbyists. Fewer households grow food than in their grandparents' day.

## Part III — Technology and information

### Decade by decade

- **1960s–1980s:** Television, radio, landlines, paper archives and industrial control systems coexist with specialised computer networks. Records can be lost through ordinary neglect as easily as through concealment.
- **1990s:** Personal computers and the public web change commerce, work and research. **CircuitPoint** opens in 1992 for enthusiasts building their own machines. Y2K planning produces documentation that historians still use.
- **2000s:** Mobile phones, digital logistics, online marketplaces and networked finance spread unevenly. **Cindrix Market**, founded in 1998, becomes a household habit.
- **2010s:** Smartphones, social platforms, cloud services and data-driven advertising open new ways to organise people and to watch them. Harrowgate makes privacy a public controversy.
- **2020–2026:** Remote work, digital public services, supply-chain anxiety and emerging AI tools sit alongside cash businesses, paper forms and people who cannot afford reliable access.

### How dependent are people?

Very, and unevenly. Many people in 2026 cannot navigate without a phone, cannot pay without a card or app, and keep their photographs, contacts and documents in the cloud. Many could not tell you how their water is treated or where their electricity comes from. This is not a moral failing; it is how specialised societies work. It is recorded here because the transition rules in later eras turn on exactly this gap between what a character relies on and what a character knows how to do.

### Radio and the hobbyists

Amateur radio, GMRS and scanner listening are niche hobbies in 2026, kept alive by preppers, storm spotters, volunteer emergency teams and retirees. **Yuki Tanabe**'s free training materials are widely used. In counties like Calder, the ORG-03 volunteer corps runs a regular radio net. Most people have never touched a radio transmitter.

## Part IV — News, entertainment and public memory

### How people get news

Most people get news through social feeds, video platforms, search and messaging groups. National newspapers and broadcasters remain influential among older and more engaged audiences. Local news has thinned badly, leaving many towns with a weekly paper, a Facebook-style community group and the diner. Disinformation travels through the same channels as careful reporting, and people often cannot tell them apart.

### Fictional media (proposed, pending collision checks)

- **The Meridian Ledger:** an independent procurement newsletter founded by former Port Meridian union researchers, read by journalists, auditors and activists.
- **Countyline Radio:** a rural regional call-in programme where farmers, truckers and retirees argue about prices, weather and local politics.
- **Repair Commons:** a volunteer maintenance publication and forum.

### Entertainment

Real-world films, books, music, games and sports provide the backdrop. People stream shows, follow teams, play video games, go to church suppers and county fairs, and argue about all of it online. A small genre of documentaries and podcasts about Harrowgate, Port Meridian and the Orion files exists in the setting (proposed); some are careful, most are sensational.

### How scandals are remembered

Public memory is not one official chronology. A union member remembers Port Meridian as lost work. An engineer remembers Cascadia as a database failure. A journalist remembers an unanswered records request. A family remembers that the alert never came. Harrowgate is a hero story, a betrayal story or a cover-up story depending on who is telling it. Characters who ask "what happened?" will get different answers, all of them partly true.

## Part V — Religion and community

### Congregations and associations

Religious congregations, secular clubs, civic associations, cooperatives, unions, sports leagues and volunteer networks give people real support: meals for the sick, childcare, rides to appointments, help after storms. In rural areas, the church, the fire hall and the school are often the three pillars of community life. No religious tradition is inherently a threat. Faith in the setting is a source of both great charity and, occasionally, control, as in any real society.

### Heritage Ranch as a community

**Heritage Ranch** is a fictional survivalist compound in rural west-central Texas, founded in 2008 on an existing 135-acre cattle ranch. It is religiously influenced and socially conservative, and for its members it is also simply home: families, work, worship, schooling and shared meals. Neighbours experience it in different ways: paid work and storm help, social distance, and concern about how much authority its leaders hold over members. Elias Mercer's founding ideal of voluntary mutual aid must be kept distinct from the stricter direction his children argue for in 2026. Players should be able to sit at a Heritage supper and find both generosity and rigidity at the same table.

### Neighbours in practice

Around the ranch, **Pastor Miriam Cole**'s independent congregation runs food drives open to everyone; **Caleb Voss**'s cooperative shares tractors and labour; **Sofia Valdez** organises county exercises and inspects volunteer groups. This web of ordinary institutions is what keeps rural communities working.

## Part VI — Preparedness as ordinary culture

### Who prepares, and why

Many people in 2026 keep a few days of water and food, a first-aid kit and a torch. Fewer keep weeks of supplies, generators or radios. Preparedness varies by budget and environment: hurricane coasts, tornado plains, wildfire hills, snowbound valleys and earthquake zones each have their own habits. The pandemic shortages of 2020–2021 pushed many households to stock up and some to go much further.

### The spectrum

- **Neighbourhood preparedness:** first-aid classes, community emergency response teams, ORG-03-style volunteer corps, church phone trees.
- **Household preparedness:** pantries, water storage, generators, emergency kits.
- **Homesteading:** gardens, chickens, canning, wood stoves; often as much a lifestyle as a plan.
- **Organised preparedness communities:** groups such as Heritage Ranch that build infrastructure, training and membership around self-reliance.

Most preppers are not political extremists. Some groups stress independence; others stress neighbourhood cooperation. The setting treats both seriously.

### Where people buy it

**Ridgeline Outfitters** for outdoor gear and expert advice; **Sentinel Supply Co.** for professional field equipment; **Harvest & Homestead** for seeds and preservation; **Ironfield Supply** for tools and generators; **CircuitPoint** for radios and electronics; and **Cindrix Market** for everything else.

## Part VII — Lives around the world

### Briar Hollow, West Virginia

A small Appalachian town with forest roads, a volunteer fire hall, a county courthouse and a family-owned diner. People here remember mine closures and the opioid crisis, and they know their neighbours. **Sheriff Helen Mercer** (no relation to the Texas Mercers) is accountable to the community; **Ruth Calder** keeps the local history. Gossip moves faster than the weekly paper.

### Calder County

In **Fenwick**, people commute to the hospital, the rail yard and county offices, shop at warehouse stores and send children to the high school. In **Alder Crossing**, life turns on the grain elevator, the feed store and the diner, and the clinic opens only two days a week.

### Aldane Coast

**Port Aldane** is a busy port city with a university and a dense downtown; its residents depend on imports and on the port's jobs. In **Kestrel Bay**, fishing families, a cannery and a volunteer rescue boat define the village, along with an old feud between boat owners.

### Ridgeback Highlands

In **Hollis Gap**, the sawmill, the dam and the pass shape every season. Winters are long, the mountain rescue team is respected, and everyone knows what happens when the pass closes.

### Manila

A dense, energetic capital where barangay officials, jeepney drivers, port workers, students and journalists share crowded streets. Typhoon season shapes life; local disaster networks are strong. Filipino characters lead their own stories.

### Nairobi

A city of technology hubs, research partnerships, matatus and markets, where local engineers design systems for local conditions and push back against investor terms that ignore them.

### London

Finance, law, universities and old neighbourhoods; a place where ownership structures are complicated and confidentiality is both lawful and abused.

## Part VIII — What 2026 carries forward

This section exists because Core sets up the other two eras. It records facts about the present, not predictions.

| Area | What most people in 2026 rely on | What fewer people know how to do |
|---|---|---|
| Food | Supermarkets, delivery, long supply chains | Growing, preserving, butchering, storing |
| Water | Treated municipal supply | Purifying, finding, storing at scale |
| Power and heat | The grid, gas, fuel deliveries | Running and repairing generators, wood heat |
| Communication | Phones, internet, messaging | Radio, written messages, local networks |
| Money | Cards, apps, bank balances | Barter, valuing goods, keeping ledgers |
| Health | Pharmacies, hospitals, clinics | First aid beyond basics, herbal and field care |
| Transport | Cars, fuel stations, roads, deliveries | Mechanical repair, animal care, navigation without GPS |
| Information | Search engines, social feeds | Verifying sources, keeping paper records |

Characters' occupations, skills and backgrounds determine where they fall in this table. The transition rules, not this history, decide what happens if the world changes around them.
