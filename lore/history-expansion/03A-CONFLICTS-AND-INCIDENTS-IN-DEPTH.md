# Blackbox Protocol: Conflicts, Scandals and Disputes
## An in-depth public register of the fault lines behind the Core world, 1970–2026

**Setting:** Blackbox Protocol (Core)
**Status:** Expanded draft. Supplements `03-CONFLICTS-AND-INCIDENTS.md` and the Core manuscript; replaces neither. The manuscript wins any conflict. Details marked **(proposed)** are new and stay editable.
**Audience:** Player-safe, as of 1 January 2026. Where the GM Secret History holds the truth of an incident, this register gives only the public course and the competing public accounts.
**Fiction notice:** Every incident here is fictional. Real places appear only as geography. Allegations remain allegations unless an in-world court or an independently established record has settled them.
**Rules notice:** Setting history only.

---

## How to use this register

### Why conflicts matter in Core

The Core world is not at war with itself. It is a functioning world full of unresolved arguments, and those arguments are where Core campaigns find their stories. Every conflict here has:

- **people with legitimate interests on more than one side;**
- **a public record that is incomplete and partly contradictory;**
- **documents and witnesses a character can actually find;**
- **routes to action that are not violent**: research, testimony, negotiation, organising, reporting, litigation, mediation.

These conflicts also set up the later eras. The arguments about who controls emergency systems, who may set conditions on help, and who keeps the records are the same arguments that, in a campaign that moves into Signal Lost, stop being debates and start being survival questions. None of that future is visible in 2026, and none of it is inevitable.

### Entry format

Each major entry follows the same shape: **Background**, **Parties**, **Trigger**, **Public course**, **Outcome**, **Competing accounts**, **What survives** (records a character can find), and **At the table**.

### Classification of claims

Following F15, this register separates:

- **Documented:** established by a court, an official finding or multiple independent records;
- **Claimed:** asserted by a party, with some support;
- **Rumoured:** circulating without credible support;
- **GM truth:** not in this document.

## Part I — The foundational argument

### The Continuity and Custody Schism, 1970s (fictional, partly public)

**Background.** Inside Project Looking Glass, two schools of thought split over a single question: should emergency systems keep operating when no lawful authority can be reached?

**Parties.** The **custodial school**, which demanded strict civilian control, documented authority and automatic expiry of emergency powers; and the **continuity school**, which argued that some essential functions had to survive even a total breakdown of institutions. Civilian legal advisers sided mostly with the custodians.

**Public course.** Nothing was public at the time. The split became known in fragments from the 1990s onward, through declassified studies, memoirs and court filings.

**Outcome.** No settlement. Some proposals were rejected, some rewritten, a few kept as theoretical contingencies.

**Competing accounts.** Reform advocates in 2026 treat the custodial school as prophets. Continuity professionals say the continuity school was solving a real problem that the custodians never answered: what to do when there is genuinely no one to ask.

**What survives.** Declassified study abstracts; two memoirs by former analysts (proposed); a phrase, "last-resort continuity authority", that appears in court filings without a definition.

**At the table.** Historians, law students and policy researchers can trace the argument back from any modern dispute. It is the root of every other entry in this register.

## Part II — The 1990s

### The Civilian Boundary Debate, 1996–1997 (fictional)

**Background.** In several countries, secret procurement for continuity and security programs was growing faster than oversight.

**Parties.** Legislators, inspectors general, civil-liberties advocates; security officials; contractors who preferred discretion.

**Trigger.** Inspectors general found secret contracts that transferred public responsibilities to private firms with little review. None of them knew about the Blackbox Initiative, but they had found real problems in programs adjacent to it.

**Public course.** Committee hearings, critical reports, a handful of press exposés and a series of procedural reforms in some jurisdictions.

**Outcome.** A patchwork. Some countries tightened review sharply; others accepted that disclosure would expose vulnerable infrastructure.

**Competing accounts.** Reformers say the debate caught problems early and was then ignored. Security officials say it showed that review could be done without disclosing everything.

**What survives.** Committee transcripts; inspector-general reports, some still partly redacted.

**At the table.** The patchwork matters in 2026: the same contractor may face strict audits in one country and almost none across a border. Characters following a record across jurisdictions run straight into it.

### The Port Meridian Contract Dispute, 1998 (fictional)

**Background.** Port Meridian, an international port, was modernising its terminals. The project was legitimate and the new terminals still operate in 2026. Emergency logistics capacity was part of the bid.

**Parties.**
- Procurement administrators and the winning infrastructure consortium;
- Losing bidders;
- Port workers and their union, led publicly by **Daniel Rusk**;
- Legal advocates and records-access campaigners;
- Public oversight bodies;
- Journalists, including a young **Elias Ward** in the dispute's later stages.

**Trigger.** Losing bidders complained that the winning consortium had information it should not have had. Workers complained that reassignments and confidentiality agreements came with the contract. Records requests were refused on security grounds.

**Public course (proposed detail).**
- **1998:** Bid protests filed. Union meetings over reassignments. First press coverage.
- **1999:** An administrative review opens. Several officials are removed from their posts. A junior analyst is publicly reported to have raised concerns internally and been blocked; the analyst is not named in the public record.
- **2000–2003:** Records-access litigation by the union and a coalition of journalists. Partial releases, heavy redactions.
- **2005–2006:** A later internal review is reported to have found the original secrecy-first response improper. The finding is summarised in public; the review itself is not released.
- **2024:** Rusk testifies at the Greybridge Hearings and calls Port Meridian "the template".

**Outcome.** Reputational damage, incomplete transparency, removal of several officials, and no full public accounting. Not all beneficiaries were held responsible.

**Competing accounts.**
- **Workers:** The dispute cost jobs and was buried to protect insiders.
- **Some former officials:** It was a contained procurement error that was dealt with internally.
- **The winning consortium:** It won fairly, and the port works.
- **Historians:** The first known case of secrecy protecting itself at workers' and the public's expense.

**What survives.** Union archives; shipping manifests; bid-protest filings; litigation records; redacted review summaries; Rusk's personal file of records requests. (GM: the decision chain and internal memoranda are in the GM Secret History.)

**At the table.** A Core character can trace a disputed subcontract, interview a retired crane operator, or find a document in the 2025 Orion leaks that appears to be the missing piece. Port Meridian is the best-documented entry point to the Blackbox legacy for players.

## Part III — The 2000s

### The Meridian Accords jurisdiction debate, 2004–2006 (fictional)

**Background.** After 2001, demand for cross-border emergency coordination rose sharply. A limited group of governments negotiated the Meridian Accords.

**Parties.** Participating governments and their legal advisers; emergency managers; infrastructure providers; parliaments that demanded review; governments that declined to join.

**Trigger.** Conflicting national requirements for cross-border assistance: whose law applies when one country's contractor restores another country's communications?

**Public course.** Negotiations on data custody, contractor responsibilities and mutual aid. Public text published in 2004. Parliamentary challenges in several countries. Withdrawals and refusals. Rival regional standards developed by non-participants.

**Outcome.** A framework that works differently in every jurisdiction. The public text is not a world treaty and does not override national law. The existence of classified annexes is public; their contents are not.

**Competing accounts.** Supporters say the Accords saved lives in later disasters. Opponents say an agreement written for emergencies became routine authority by stealth.

**What survives.** The public text; parliamentary debates; legal commentary; references in later hearings.

**At the table.** Lawyers, diplomats and journalists can work on the question every annex rumour raises: what did governments actually agree to?

### The Auditable Logistics campaign, 2005–2010 (fictional, proposed name)

**Background.** Hurricane Katrina (real, 2005) showed how badly emergency logistics could fail when no one could reconstruct who sent what, where.

**Parties.** Professional associations of emergency managers and logisticians; contractors; budget officials; academics including **Dr. Amara Bell**.

**Trigger.** Post-disaster reviews found that supply records could not be reconciled.

**Public course.** Professional standards drafted; model contract clauses published; adoption by some agencies and states; resistance from contractors who called it costly.

**Outcome.** Partial success. Auditable logistics became best practice on paper and patchy in reality.

**What survives.** Model clauses; association journals; Bell's early comparative studies.

**At the table.** Any modern supply dispute can be investigated by asking whether the contract included these clauses and whether anyone enforced them.

### The Accountability Wave, 2009–2010 (fictional)

**Background.** The financial crisis and rapid consolidation of contractors made public anger at insulated firms intense.

**Parties.** Legislative committees in several countries; inspectors general; contractors, Asterion-financed acquisitions among them; security officials.

**Public course.** Reviews of contracting, data retention and emergency powers. Findings of real waste and conflicts of interest. Procurement reforms. At the same time, real cyber incidents of the period showed how exposed connected infrastructure was, and security officials warned against dismantling networks without replacements.

**Outcome.** Real reforms and an unresolved contradiction: people distrust the systems they depend on.

**At the table.** A source of committee transcripts, retired auditors and reformed contract terms that characters can check against practice.

## Part IV — The 2010s

### The Cascadia Continuity Review, 2012 (fictional)

**Background.** A major earthquake-response exercise in the Pacific Northwest tested public-private emergency planning against a simulated Cascadia Subduction Zone event.

**Parties.** Regional officials in the US and Canada; public utilities; technical experts; contractors; tribal nations and civic groups in affected areas (proposed); volunteer radio teams.

**Trigger.** Concern about catastrophic earthquake preparedness and communication interoperability.

**Public course.** The exercise ran over several days. Contractor-supported logistics moved medical supplies quickly to some places. Incompatible databases delayed help to isolated communities. A public review followed.

**Outcome.** Recommendations for open interoperability standards and stronger local authority; several adopted. An unresolved dispute over who controlled private technical records generated during the exercise.

**Competing accounts.** Contractors cite the speed of their deliveries. Local officials cite the communities their systems missed. Both are right.

**What survives.** Engineering papers; budget hearings; local preparedness plans; after-action reports; volunteer radio logs.

**At the table.** The clearest public lesson of the era, used by emergency managers in every campaign region. Characters with emergency-services backgrounds may have trained on it.

### The Contractor-Access Investigations, 2013 (fictional, set against real surveillance debates)

**Background.** Real public debates about intelligence surveillance in 2013 had fictional counterparts in the setting.

**Parties.** Independent journalists, legal advocates, contractors, agencies.

**Public course.** Investigations into contractor access to information collected for security and infrastructure purposes. Specific agencies and contracts examined.

**Outcome.** Some allegations substantiated; others disproven. Public trust damaged because legitimate secrecy made verification so hard.

**At the table.** A useful lesson for players: not every allegation is true, and disproven claims leave records too.

### The Harrowgate Affair, 2016 (fictional)

**Background.** A contractor operated a Blackbox-related system under authorisation limited to specific continuity purposes.

**Parties.**
- **Nadia Serrano**, a technical auditor who preserved access logs;
- The contractor and individuals within it who carried out unlawful monitoring;
- Affected civilians;
- Investigators, prosecutors and legal counsel;
- Journalists, led by **Elias Ward**;
- Oversight officials and legislators.

**Trigger.** Serrano's preserved logs showed civilians being monitored far beyond lawful authority. She reported through protected channels; when they did not act in time, she went to independent investigators.

**Public course (proposed detail).**
- **Spring 2016:** Ward's corroborated series begins.
- **Summer 2016:** Hearings open. Officials resign.
- **2016–2018:** Criminal prosecutions of several individuals; civil suits by affected civilians.
- **2017:** Reforms restrict access, require better audit trails and create independent reporting obligations.
- **2017 onward:** Fabricated "Harrowgate documents" circulate; Ward publishes a guide to spotting them.

**Outcome.** Documented: the contractor's misconduct, several convictions, the existence of several classified continuity arrangements. Some accusations dismissed. Not documented publicly: the full structure linking those arrangements to the 1991 Initiative.

**Competing accounts.**
- **Reformers:** Proof that oversight works when someone is brave enough.
- **Security advocates:** Proof that a few bad actors can be removed without dismantling essential capabilities.
- **Conspiracy circles:** Proof of everything they already believed, usually with forged documents attached.
- **Affected civilians:** A violation that compensation did not undo.

**What survives.** Court records; hearing transcripts; Ward's series; reform statutes; the forgery guide; Serrano's testimony.

**At the table.** The most famous fictional scandal of the Core era. Almost every adult has an opinion. A fair distinction between careful reporting and fabrication is built into it.

### The Fragmentation Audit, 2017 (fictional)

**Background.** After Harrowgate, oversight bodies wanted a complete inventory of what had been built under the old continuity arrangements.

**Parties.** Auditors; archival custodians; technical operators; oversight bodies; reformers; continuity professionals.

**Trigger.** Inconsistent record custody and inherited emergency permissions discovered during Harrowgate remediation.

**Public course.** A classified multi-agency audit. Its headline finding became public through official summaries: no single authority could produce a complete inventory of systems descended from the original Initiative.

**Outcome.** Some remediation and decommissioning. Disputed residual risks. The audit explicitly rejected the idea that one hidden organisation commands the network.

**Competing accounts.** Reformers want everything disclosed and retired. Technical staff fear that hasty decommissioning will break services people need. Conspiracy theorists say the audit's denial proves the opposite.

**What survives.** Public summaries; later hearing references; decommissioning notices.

**At the table.** An institutional mystery with mundane explanations and real misconduct side by side. Characters who find an unlisted facility in 2026 may simply have found a forgotten one.

### The Legitimacy Debate, 2018–2019 (fictional, set against real trends)

**Parties.** Protest movements, electoral campaigns, investigators, unions, reform groups, agencies and companies.

**Public course.** Accountability became a central political issue in several countries. Disinformation and genuine reporting travelled through the same channels.

**Outcome.** Contract cancellations and better disclosure rules. Some cooperation, some resistance. A tense world that is not uniformly deteriorating.

## Part V — The 2020s

### The Strait Lantern Disruption, 2022 (fictional)

**Background.** A busy Asia-Pacific shipping corridor depends on maritime communications and navigation systems.

**Parties.** Shipping companies; fishing communities; coast guards and navies of the Philippines, Japan and neighbouring states; investigators; insurers; reporters including **Rina Mercado**.

**Trigger.** Maritime communications along the corridor were temporarily interrupted.

**Public course.** Vessels lost communications and some navigation services. Coast guards improvised. Investigations considered technical failure, criminal interference and state-sponsored action.

**Outcome.** No universally accepted cause. Investment in maritime safety, emergency communications and independent verification.

**Competing accounts.** Each government and each industry has a preferred explanation. Fishing communities remember who helped them and who did not.

**What survives.** Investigation reports from several countries, which disagree; insurance claims; coast guard logs; Mercado's reporting.

**At the table.** A live, unsolved international incident and an entry point for Philippine and wider Asia-Pacific campaigns.

### The Sovereignty Reviews, 2023 (fictional emphasis, real trends)

**Parties.** National legislatures and regulators; African regional institutions; Latin American governments, unions and port authorities; European procurement bodies; foreign-linked contractors; engineers such as **Adewale Bako**.

**Public course.** Reviews of foreign ownership of critical infrastructure and access to sensitive data. Tougher negotiating terms in parts of Africa. Debates over ports, telecommunications and organised crime in Latin America. Data protection and industrial policy in Europe.

**Outcome.** Varies by country. No region is a mere battleground for outside powers.

**At the table.** International campaigns about contracts, ownership and local control.

### The Greybridge Hearings, 2024 (fictional)

**Background.** Two decades of scandals and reforms had left a basic question unanswered.

**Parties.** Legislators in several countries; affected workers including **Daniel Rusk**; professional witnesses; public-interest advocates; corporate representatives.

**Trigger.** Demands for transparent emergency contracting and information governance, fuelled by revolving-door appointments and improper data access.

**Public course.** Months of testimony in several parliaments and congresses, reported internationally under one name.

**Outcome.** Misconduct found; effective oversight also found. Some companies lost contracts; others were cleared. The hearings did not reveal the Blackbox Protocol as a single coherent organisation. They left open the question that defines 2026 politics: reduce dependence on private continuity systems even at temporary risk, or reform them while keeping them running?

**What survives.** Public transcripts, which characters can consult; committee reports; press coverage.

**At the table.** A source for nearly any Core investigation. A character who reads the transcripts closely will find contradictions between witnesses.

### The Orion Data Dispute, 2025 onward (fictional)

**Background.** A technology contractor went out of business years earlier. Its records were supposed to have been archived or destroyed.

**Parties.** Independent journalists, including **Elias Ward**; government investigators; corporate compliance teams; criminal brokers selling files; forgers.

**Trigger.** Records from the defunct contractor surfaced in several countries at once.

**Public course.** Some files concern ordinary procurement. Others appear to reference obsolete Blackbox access procedures. Provenance is incomplete, and forgeries circulate alongside authentic material. A race to authenticate.

**Outcome.** Unresolved on 1 January 2026. No one holds the full archive.

**At the table.** The freshest mystery of the era and the easiest one for characters to fall into. A character might be offered a file, asked to verify one, or find that one names them.

## Part VI — Local conflicts: Heritage Ranch

### The road and boundary dispute, 2016–2019 (fictional)

**Background.** As Heritage Ranch grew, its traffic, gates and access procedures began to affect neighbouring independent farms.

**Parties.** Nathan Mercer and the ranch's security staff; **Caleb Voss** and the ranching cooperative; neighbouring landholders; county officials.

**Public course.** Complaints at county meetings about gates on shared roads, visitor checks and truck traffic. Mediated discussions.

**Outcome.** Partial agreements; lingering resentment.

**Competing accounts.** Nathan: the ranch was protecting its property and families. Voss: the ranch was treating shared roads as its own.

**What survives.** County meeting minutes; cooperative correspondence.

**At the table.** A mundane but revealing dispute. It shows a philosophical divide between private preparedness and control that matters far more than its subject.

### The pandemic recruitment debate, 2020–2022 (fictional)

**Parties.** Heritage Ranch leadership; new families seeking safety; neighbours; local media; **Pastor Miriam Cole**.

**Public course.** The ranch helped neighbours during shortages while keeping restrictive access rules. Recruitment rose. Positive accounts of practical help appeared alongside criticism that leaders were using public anxiety to recruit.

**Outcome.** No resolution. Elias publicly insisted that help must never require conversion or submission.

### The 2024 preparedness forum (fictional)

**Public course.** Elias, Nathan and Rebecca shared a stage at a regional preparedness forum. Elias praised voluntary mutual aid; Nathan called for stronger perimeter and supply-chain control; Rebecca argued that membership carries obligations beyond those of a club or church.

**Outcome.** Local coverage noted their differences without predicting anything.

**What survives.** Video and audio of the forum; local press reports.

### The access and membership controversy, 2025 (fictional)

**Background.** Several former participants publicly criticised restrictions and internal disciplinary practices.

**Parties.** Mercer leadership; former residents; neighbouring landholders; regional officials including **Sofia Valdez**.

**Public course.** Local reporting; ranch rebuttals disputing key parts of the accounts; community meetings.

**Outcome.** Conflicting accounts; no finding of criminal guilt. Any legal findings need a future local dossier.

**At the table.** A playable dispute with witnesses who have different interests. A character can investigate responsibly, mediate, report or simply listen.

## Common threads

Across every entry, the same six fault lines repeat. They are listed in the Core manuscript and are the arguments a campaign carries forward into later eras if it goes there:

1. **Contractor authority versus public accountability.**
2. **Resilience versus dependency.**
3. **Property rights versus community obligations.**
4. **Public safety versus civil liberties.**
5. **Mutual aid versus territorial control.**
6. **National interests versus cross-border coordination.**
