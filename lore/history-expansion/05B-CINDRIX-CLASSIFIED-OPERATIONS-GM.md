# Cindrix: Classified Operations
## GM-only companion to the Core corporate history

**Setting:** Blackbox Protocol (Core)
**Audience:** **GM only.** Nothing in this file is public knowledge on 1 January 2026. Players learn of it only through play.
**Status:** Approved direction from Jason (2026-11-26): Cindrix's security and contracting arm took part in classified operations, including fictional conflicts that resolved serious global threats before the public ever heard of them. **Every operation, name, date and detail below is proposed** and must be reconciled with `lore/core/BLACKBOX-PROTOCOL-GM-SECRET-HISTORY.md` before it is treated as canon.
**Fiction notice:** All operations, places marked fictional, and people are invented. Real countries appear only as broad geography where unavoidable, and no real government, military unit, service member, agency or company is implicated in any fictional wrongdoing. Where a real state would otherwise be implied, the location is left as an unnamed fictional or "successor" state.
**Rules notice:** Setting history only.

## How to use this file

### The canonical frame

The GM Secret History is clear: Blackbox is a fragmented lineage of programs, agreements, systems, people and records, **not** a single secret agency, a shadow government or a corporation. Cindrix is **influence without omnipotence**.

These operations fit inside that frame:

- **Governments authorised them.** Each was commissioned by one or more allied governments under classified contracts. Cindrix supplied people, logistics, communications and security. It did not decide on its own to go to war.
- **They really did avert crises.** Each operation ended a threat, such as loose weapons material, a hostage crisis or a regional escalation, before it became a public event. That is why nobody heard about them.
- **They had costs.** People died, laws were stretched, records were sealed, and some contractors did things they should not have. Success and wrongdoing sit side by side, as everywhere else in Blackbox history.
- **Some used Blackbox-derived authorities.** Several operations relied on communications standards, emergency permissions or record channels inherited from the Initiative. That is how Cindrix units ended up holding legacy components and credentials (see the GM Secret History, section 7).

### Why this matters for the eras

Core is the era that sets up the other two. These operations leave behind trained people, sealed records, hidden caches, informal loyalties and favours owed, all of which are useful hooks in Core. They are not predictions about Signal Lost or Dead Zone.

## Part I — The origin: from the Gulf to the shadows, 1991–1993

During the 1990–1991 Gulf War, Cindrix's contractors earned a reputation with allied planners for moving communications gear and secure materiel quickly, quietly and under fire. After the war, a small number of officials with continuity and security responsibilities began using Cindrix teams for work that governments did not want to do with uniformed forces or acknowledge publicly.

**GM truth (proposed):** In 1992, a classified contract created a small unit inside what would later become **Cindrix Operations**. Internally it was called **Field Services Group 7**, usually shortened to **"Seven"**. It was staffed by former special-operations, signals, logistics and medical personnel hired as independent contractors. On paper it repaired communications equipment. In practice it ran deniable support missions for its government clients.

**Open variable:** Whether "Seven" was formally linked to the Blackbox Initiative or simply used the same contracting channels. The default is the latter: same channels, separate authority.

## Part II — The operations

### Operation Ashfall, 1994 (proposed)

**The threat.** After the Soviet dissolution, a depot in an unnamed successor state held poorly secured radiological material and the paperwork for it was a mess. Intelligence reported that a criminal network had bribed guards and was arranging a sale.

**The operation.** Under a classified contract from allied governments, Seven provided transport, secure communications and a recovery team that moved the material to a safe facility over three nights, working with local officials who wanted it gone. Cindrix trucks and a chartered cargo aircraft did the moving.

**Outcome.** The sale never happened. The material was secured. The public never knew there had been a threat.

**Cost and controversy.** Two local guards died in a confrontation with the criminal network's couriers. Their families were told of a road accident. A Cindrix operator disobeyed a stand-down order to pull a wounded colleague out, and was quietly let go.

**What survives.** A sealed after-action report; a cargo manifest listing "communications spares"; the dismissed operator, now in his sixties, living near Clarksville and working as a mechanic (proposed NPC: **Tom Brannigan**).

**At the table.** A veteran in a VFW hall who will not talk about 1994. A shipping record that does not add up.

### Operation Saltline, 1999 (proposed)

**The threat.** A maritime standoff between two navies in a fictional strait, the **Kessane Strait**, was escalating after a collision. Both sides' communications were failing, and each misread the other's movements. Planners feared a shooting incident would follow within days.

**The operation.** Seven deployed a communications team under civilian cover as a "marine electronics contractor" to restore a neutral relay link that let both sides' commanders talk directly, a small-scale version of the 1963 hotline lesson. It also evacuated foreign engineers from an offshore platform caught between the fleets.

**Outcome.** The standoff de-escalated within a week. Public reporting described "tense days" and a diplomatic resolution.

**Cost and controversy.** The relay used a Blackbox-era interoperability standard that Cindrix was not authorised to deploy abroad. The technical team kept the equipment afterwards. This is one origin of the obsolete restricted component mentioned in the GM Secret History.

**What survives.** Relay hardware in a Cindrix storage facility, logged as legacy stock; a commendation letter to the team leader that names no operation.

**At the table.** An ORG-01 or Cindrix technician finds hardware with markings no one recognises.

### Operation Northlight, 2006 (proposed)

**The threat.** A trafficking network was buying dual-use electronic components, some through legitimate supply chains, including Cindrix's own distribution channels, and selling them to an armed group planning attacks on civilian infrastructure.

**The operation.** Working with investigators from several allied governments, Seven ran a sting. It supplied deliberately flawed components through a front company, traced the shipments and handed the network's leaders to authorities in three countries.

**Outcome.** The planned attacks never took place. Several arrests were reported publicly as ordinary customs prosecutions, without mention of Cindrix.

**Cost and controversy.** Running a sting through its own supply chain meant Cindrix knowingly sold to criminals for eighteen months. One shipment went missing and was never recovered. Internal lawyers objected; the contract went ahead anyway.

**What survives.** The front company's records, which surfaced partially in the **2025 Orion Data Dispute** files (proposed link); a missing shipment of components somewhere.

**At the table.** An Orion file mentions a company that never existed. The missing shipment turns up in a warehouse in Calder County or Port Aldane.

### Operation Quiet Ledger, 2014 (proposed)

**The threat.** Engineers maintaining a regional power and water system in a fictional city, **Sarai**, in an unnamed country, were taken hostage by an armed faction threatening to sabotage the systems, which would have cut water and power to more than a million people.

**The operation.** Seven organised the engineers' release through local intermediaries, with a contingency team standing by, and brought in replacement crews to keep the systems running. No shots were fired.

**Outcome.** The engineers were freed; the systems stayed up. Local authorities took public credit, as agreed.

**Cost and controversy.** A ransom was paid through a chain of intermediaries, in breach of several governments' stated policies. The payment was disguised as a maintenance contract.

**What survives.** A maintenance invoice for work never done; grateful engineers, one of whom later spoke at a conference alongside **Adewale Bako**, and almost said too much.

**At the table.** A conference recording with an odd, cut-off answer. An invoice that does not match any work order.

## Part III — Seven in 2026

**GM truth (proposed):** Seven was formally dissolved after the 2016 Harrowgate Affair, when Cindrix's lawyers concluded that its contracts could not survive the new audit-trail rules. Its people went three ways:

- **Retired or moved on.** Most former operators live ordinary lives, bound by NDAs and loyalty to each other.
- **Absorbed.** Some now work openly in Cindrix Operations, Cindrix Response and Cindrix Security, doing legitimate work, and know how the old jobs were done.
- **Unaccounted for.** A few left with equipment, contacts and grievances. At least one sells their skills privately.

The records sit in several places: sealed government archives, Cindrix's legal department, private notebooks, and, in fragments, the Orion files.

### Inside Cindrix today

- One **compliance manager** (see GM Secret History, section 7) wants all legacy material decommissioned and documented, and suspects there is more of it than anyone admits.
- One **executive** resists because disclosure would expose old contracts and old favours.
- Most employees know nothing.

### Rules for running Seven

- **No omnipotence.** Seven was a contractor unit, not a secret government. It succeeded sometimes and failed sometimes.
- **No real-world blame.** No real state, unit, agency or person is implicated.
- **Evidence over insinuation.** Every hook above leads to something testable: a manifest, an invoice, a witness, a piece of hardware.
- **Moral weight both ways.** These operations saved lives. They also broke laws and buried deaths. Players should be able to argue either side.
