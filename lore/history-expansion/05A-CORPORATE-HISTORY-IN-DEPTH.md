# Blackbox Protocol: The Companies That Built the Present
## An in-depth public corporate and institutional history, 1956–2026

**Setting:** Blackbox Protocol (Core)
**Status:** Expanded draft. Supplements `05-CORPORATE-HISTORY.md`, `notes/CORPORATE-AND-RETAIL-GUIDE.md` and `notes/ORGANIZATIONS.md`; replaces none of them. Those sources win any conflict, and this draft does not change any established division, founding date, ownership or mechanic. Items marked **(proposed)** are new and editable.
**Audience:** Player-safe, as of 1 January 2026. Confidential programs that the corporate guide mentions are referred to only as things the public knows exist, never described.
**Fiction notice:** Every company here is fictional. No founder, executive or real company is named or implied. Approved founding dates are used; unapproved ones are left open.
**Rules notice:** Setting history only. Nothing here sets prices, stock, delivery times, membership benefits, faction statistics or clocks.

---

## How to use this history

### Why companies matter in Core

In the Core world, most people meet the Blackbox legacy, if they ever do, through companies rather than governments. The parcel that arrives on time, the pharmacy that fills the prescription, the insurer that pays or refuses, the contractor that keeps the county alert system running: these are the institutions that shape daily life in 2026. They are also where the setting's central argument, between resilience and dependency, becomes concrete.

### Three principles

1. **No company is a mandatory villain.** Each has decent employees, useful products and real failings. Ordinary employees are not presumed complicit in anything.
2. **A contract is not a conspiracy.** Cindrix can distribute Veydran products; Asterion can finance projects involving either. That proves nothing about secret alliances.
3. **Dependency is the theme.** Core is the era that builds the dependencies later eras test. This history shows how those dependencies formed without saying what happens to them.

### Open questions this draft does not settle

- **Founders and executives** of Cindrix, Veydran and Asterion are unapproved. This history names none.
- **The parent corporation's founding date.** The Core manuscript says Cindrix "begins its ascent" in 1993–1994 through communications, systems integration and infrastructure services. The corporate guide approves **1998** as the founding year of **Cindrix Market** and says the retailer laid the foundation for the wider empire. This draft treats them as compatible: an early systems and services business in the 1990s, and the 1998 marketplace that became the brand's public face and growth engine. The reconciliation is flagged at the end for a decision.
- **Founding dates of Veydran and Asterion** are unapproved and left open.

## Part I — The landscape before the divergence, 1956–1990

### The old economy

Before 1991, the Core world's economy looks like ours. Utilities are mostly public or tightly regulated. Telephone networks are national monopolies or near-monopolies. Retail is local and regional, with mail-order catalogues for the countryside. Government emergency planning is done by government.

Two of the setting's established retailers date from this period:

- **Ironfield Supply (founded 1956)** begins as a regional hardware and agricultural machinery supplier. It grows with post-war construction and manufacturing, buying smaller hardware stores and industrial distributors. By the 1980s it is the place tradespeople, mechanics and farmers go for parts. Its reputation for durability and parts availability is built here.
- **Ridgeline Outfitters (founded 1978)** starts as a small outdoor shop run by two wilderness guides for hikers, hunters and backcountry travellers. It sells field-tested equipment through knowledgeable staff and grows slowly and deliberately.
- **Harvest & Homestead (founded 1983)** begins as a mail-order seed and agricultural supply business serving gardeners and small farms through a catalogue that many rural households keep for years.

**What this means in 2026.** These companies predate the contractor economy and have a different relationship with the public. Older customers trust them as the shops their parents used.

### The continuity planners and their contractors

Through the Looking Glass years and the dormant Blackbox Architecture, emergency planning relies on ordinary contractors: telephone engineers, defence electronics firms, construction companies. Nothing in the public record ties any specific company to Looking Glass. What is known is that the planners kept returning to one idea: private operators of telecommunications, data, logistics and medical supply might matter as much to continuity as government offices.

## Part II — The contractor economy, 1991–2000

### Demobilisation and a new market

The end of the Cold War releases engineers, analysts, logistics officers and security professionals into civilian markets. New companies form; established firms buy specialist divisions; investors move money into dual-use technology. Governments and allied programs, including the classified Blackbox Initiative, begin awarding long-term responsibilities to private firms that would once have stayed inside agencies.

The public sees this as a boom in outsourcing. It does not see the classified frameworks behind some of the contracts.

### Cindrix: from systems to marketplace

**Early 1990s.** Cindrix rises through communications, systems integration and infrastructure services. It wins public praise for restoring communications during regional emergencies, a reputation it still uses in its advertising.

**1998 — Cindrix Market.** Cindrix launches **Cindrix Market** as an online general-merchandise retailer, one of the early internet stores. Competitive pricing, acquisitions and heavy investment in distribution make it grow fast. The distribution network it builds becomes the backbone of everything Cindrix does afterwards.

**Public debates of the period.** Small retailers complain about predatory pricing. Labour advocates question warehouse working conditions. Customers love the convenience.

### Veydran: from research to medicine

Veydran expands through research in biotechnology, advanced materials and medical systems. Its partnerships with universities and hospitals produce real medical advances. The public debates that will follow it for thirty years begin early: intellectual-property control versus access to treatment, research speed versus informed consent, public grant conditions versus confidential commercial work.

### Asterion: capital as influence

Asterion builds a network of financing, investment and strategic acquisitions. Some regulators scrutinise its methods from the start. Its influence comes from ownership stakes and financing rather than any consumer brand, so most people never notice it.

### The retailers in the 1990s

- **CircuitPoint (founded 1992)** opens as a computer-components retailer and builds a loyal following among enthusiasts, technicians and small businesses as personal computing spreads.
- **Ironfield Supply** goes public during the period (proposed date; its publicly traded status is approved) and keeps buying regional hardware chains.

### Port Meridian and the first labour questions, 1998

The Port Meridian Contract Dispute is remembered as a procurement scandal, but for workers it was a labour dispute. Reassignments and confidentiality agreements arrived with the winning consortium. The union's fight for records access, led publicly by **Daniel Rusk**, set a pattern that runs through the next three decades: workers asking what their employer's emergency contracts actually require of them.

### Y2K: the contractors' triumph

The millennium transition is the contractor economy's great public success. Utilities, banks, airlines, governments and technology firms spend years fixing date problems, and when the date turns very little goes wrong. Supporters of private continuity planning cite it as proof the model works. Critics say it entrenched private operators in public functions.

## Part III — Security and consolidation, 2001–2010

### The security boom

After 2001, private security, logistics, communications and intelligence-support industries grow rapidly. Governments depend more heavily on private systems for coordination and technical services. Oversight reviews find inconsistent standards for contractor access to sensitive information.

**Sentinel Supply Co. (founded 2004)** is born in this climate, founded by former emergency services personnel and civilian contractors. It supplies emergency responders, security firms and government agencies, and later expands into civilian sales of protective clothing, communications, medical supplies and field gear. Some of its private security relationships attract scrutiny; most of its business is ordinary.

### Cindrix becomes infrastructure

Between 2007 and 2015, Cindrix expands into cloud, logistics and business services. **Cindrix Cloud**, **Cindrix Logistics**, **Cindrix Business** and **Cindrix Pay** become part of how other companies operate, not just how consumers shop (exact launch years proposed as falling in this window). Competitors and regulators argue over whether efficiency justifies the concentration of data and distribution capacity. Cindrix divisions sometimes disagree with each other on policy.

### The financial crisis and Asterion's moment

The 2007–2009 crisis shrinks most sectors while emergency logistics and communications contracts stay stable. Well-capitalised firms buy smaller specialist contractors and their long-term government agreements. **Asterion** expands significantly through acquisitions and restructuring. Some of its deals preserve jobs and essential services; others concentrate ownership and weaken competition. Public anger grows at contractors who seem insulated from the risks households face.

### The accountability wave

Legislative reviews in 2009 and 2010 find real waste and conflicts of interest among contractors and push procurement reforms. Companies that cooperate gain reputations for responsibility; companies that resist gain scrutiny. All three majors appear in hearings during this period in some capacity (proposed), none of them as defendants in the public record.

## Part IV — The age of influence, 2011–2019

### Everyday dependence

Smartphones, cloud services and digital payments make Cindrix part of daily life for millions. **Cindrix Plus** memberships, same-day delivery in cities, **Cindrix Fresh** groceries and **Cindrix Health** pharmacies (launch years proposed) mean that many households now depend on a single company for a large share of what they buy. Rural areas and low-income neighbourhoods are served less well, which becomes its own political issue.

### Corporate rivalries

From 2014 to 2015, the three majors compete aggressively for contracts, talent and influence:

- **Cindrix** moves into infrastructure and communications management through **Cindrix Communications**, **Cindrix Infrastructure** and **Cindrix Energy**.
- **Veydran** strengthens its medical and materials research networks and competes with **Cindrix Health** in pharmacy and medical supply.
- **Asterion** finances projects across energy, logistics and technology, sometimes funding competitors of its own portfolio companies at the same time.

The same company can be a trusted employer in one town and a troubling political influence in another.

### Harrowgate and the contractors

The 2016 Harrowgate Affair centres on a contractor operating a Blackbox-related system. The public record identifies that contractor and the individuals prosecuted; it does not implicate Cindrix, Veydran or Asterion, and this history does not either. The affair nonetheless changes the whole industry. New audit-trail requirements, access restrictions and independent reporting duties raise compliance costs for every firm in the sector. Compliance departments grow. Professionals like **Leena Okafor** and **Nadia Serrano** become consultants to firms trying to avoid being the next Harrowgate.

### The Fragmentation Audit and corporate custody

The 2017 audit finds that some systems descended from the original Initiative passed through corporate acquisitions and could no longer be traced. For companies, this means awkward questions: did a division acquired in 2008 inherit obligations its new owner never knew about? Several firms quietly commission their own internal reviews (proposed). Results are not public.

### The independent retailers hold their ground

While Cindrix grows, the independents survive by being specialists:

- **Ridgeline Outfitters** resists several acquisition attempts and stays independent, trading on knowledgeable staff and its **Trailforge** house brand.
- **Harvest & Homestead** grows as interest in gardening, self-sufficiency and preparedness rises, keeping close ties to regional producers and cooperatives. Its **Hearthroot** line becomes a fixture in rural households.
- **Ironfield Supply** remains the tradesperson's store, with its **Hardline Tools** line.
- **CircuitPoint** expands into professional computing, networking, storage and diagnostics, but depends heavily on international manufacturing.
- **Sentinel Supply Co.** grows on institutional contracts and the civilian preparedness market, with its **Vanguard Field Systems** brand.

## Part V — The years of strain, 2020–2025

### The pandemic test

The 2020 pandemic is the largest public test of corporate continuity in the setting's history.

- **Cindrix Logistics** and **Cindrix Market** keep goods moving to many households and become, for a time, essential infrastructure. Warehouse working conditions, price gouging by marketplace sellers and data collection all draw scrutiny.
- **Veydran** and its competitors contribute to medical research; individual contracts and procurement decisions face scrutiny. There is no canonical claim that any company engineered the disease or caused the pandemic.
- **Asterion** finances recovery projects and acquires distressed businesses. Some of those deals save companies; others are criticised as opportunistic.
- **Harvest & Homestead** and **Ridgeline** see record demand for seeds, preservation supplies and outdoor equipment.
- **Sentinel Supply** sees institutional orders surge for protective equipment.
- **CircuitPoint** struggles with component shortages as international supply chains seize up.

Supply-chain fragility becomes a household phrase.

### Recovery and internal argument

In 2021, Cindrix promotes resilient communications systems; Veydran pursues medical and environmental research; Asterion finances recovery. Inside each company, people argue over pricing, access, governance and political influence. Former employees write memoirs; some are flattering and some are not.

### Sovereignty and the hearings

The 2023 Sovereignty Reviews put foreign-linked contractors under pressure across several countries. Cindrix Global and Asterion-financed projects face tougher terms in some markets. Engineers such as **Adewale Bako** publicly challenge proprietary systems only their vendors can maintain.

The 2024 Greybridge Hearings examine emergency contracting, revolving-door appointments and improper data access. Some companies lose contracts; others are cleared. The hearings leave open whether to reduce dependence on private continuity systems or reform them while keeping them running.

### The Orion contractor

The 2025 Orion Data Dispute begins with records from **a defunct technology contractor** that went out of business years earlier (name pending; deliberately not one of the companies above). Some of its files concern ordinary procurement; others appear to reference obsolete Blackbox access procedures. Corporate compliance teams race to find out whether any of the files mention their companies.

## Part VI — The corporate landscape on 1 January 2026

### Cindrix Corporation (ORG-04)

**Slogan:** *Everything. Within reach.*
**Influence through dependency.** A nationwide commerce, logistics, communications, infrastructure, security and civilian-contracting group with international reach. Its public identity stresses convenience, innovation, safety, employment and reliability.

**Divisions (approved):** Cindrix Market, Plus, Essentials, Logistics, Business, Cloud, Pay, Fresh, Health, Global, Communications, Systems, Financial, Energy, Infrastructure, Industries, Properties, Agriculture, Security, Operations, Intelligence, Response, Defense, Research, Advanced Systems and BioScience.

**What the public knows.** Cindrix Market is a household name, relied on by millions of customers and by smaller sellers. Its logistics are indispensable in emergencies. Its market dominance, data collection and treatment of competitors are constant controversies. **Cindrix Operations** supplies search and rescue, expedition support, infrastructure assessment, investigations, disaster response, security contracting, technical recovery and humanitarian logistics, and hires people with exactly the skills player characters tend to have. **Cindrix Advanced Systems** is publicly known to hold classified contracts; what they are is not public.

**Reputation:** Convenient, affordable, dependable, increasingly monopolistic.

**What Core sets up.** Millions of households depend on Cindrix for goods, payments, communications and, in some counties, utilities. That dependency is the company's strength. The corporate guide notes, as a possibility rather than a prediction, that its distribution hubs and trained staff could matter enormously if systems ever degraded.

**Not to be confused with** **ORG-01**, the regional infrastructure contractor whose optional name is CindrixOps. No relationship between them is established.

### Veydran Group

**Influence through knowledge.** A national scientific and medical powerhouse in biotechnology, pharmaceuticals, genetics, medical technology, advanced materials and experimental science.

**What the public knows.** Veydran's public research and healthcare partnerships have produced real treatments. It also maintains confidential facilities and programs, as many research companies do. Its long-running tensions over patents, access, consent and grant conditions remain live. Ethical researchers and exploitative managers can work in the same building.

**What Core sets up.** Advanced medicine depends on specialised supply chains and skilled people. Veydran is where much of that capacity sits.

### Asterion Holdings

**Influence through ownership.** Investment, banking, insurance, media ownership, lobbying, real estate and institutional financing.

**What the public knows.** Less recognisable than Cindrix; its power comes through stakes and loans. Its holdings should be traced through actual legal entities, not an imagined master ledger. It finances hospitals, factories and utilities, and sometimes their competitors.

**What Core sets up.** Debt, property claims and ownership records that tie together a great deal of the economy.

### Regional organisations

- **ORG-01, regional infrastructure contractor (optional name CindrixOps).** Operates utilities, communications and control systems for local governments under multi-year contracts. In Calder County it holds the utility and alert contract; on the Aldane Coast, water and port controls; in the Ridgeback Highlands, the dam and relay. Its crews know their systems intimately.
- **ORG-02, private security and logistics.** Guards, transport and warehousing. Uneasy relations with local police; sometimes hires ORG-03 volunteers.
- **ORG-03, civic emergency volunteer network.** Shelters, first aid and radio. Trusted by residents; borrows equipment from ORG-01 and ORG-02.

### Faction templates

The setting's four faction templates, local government, community cooperative, trade guild and armed group, already exist in Core in recognisable forms: town councils, farm co-ops such as **Caleb Voss**'s, business associations and private security firms. ORGANIZATIONS.md describes how each changes in later eras; that is not repeated here.

## Working in the corporate world

Characters in Core may work for these companies, compete with them, investigate them or depend on them. Useful facts for play:

- **Employees vary.** A Cindrix warehouse worker, a Veydran lab technician and an Asterion analyst live very different lives, and most of them know nothing about classified contracts.
- **Records exist.** Company filings, court cases, regulatory dockets, hearing transcripts, union records and leaked internal memos are all available to investigators.
- **Reputations are local.** A company praised for storm response in one county may be resented for pricing in the next.
- **Rivalries are real.** Disputes over data access, medical distribution, patents, insurance exposure, procurement and emergency prioritisation are common, and none of them proves a secret alliance.

## Reconciliation register for this draft

**Used as established:** all Cindrix divisions and slogan; Cindrix Market 1998; all retailer founding years, ownership and house brands; Veydran and Asterion identities; ORG-01 to ORG-03 and the optional CindrixOps name without a Cindrix link; non-villain principle; no founders or executives named.

**Needs a decision:** whether the Cindrix parent corporation existed before 1998 (Core manuscript, 1993–1994) or grew out of Cindrix Market (corporate guide). This draft assumes both: an early systems business, with the 1998 marketplace as its growth engine.

**New and proposed:** Ironfield's listing in the 1990s; 2007–2015 launch window for Cloud, Logistics, Business and Pay; launch window for Plus, Fresh and Health in the 2010s; all three majors appearing in accountability-wave hearings without being defendants; internal post-audit reviews; pandemic effects on each retailer; the Orion contractor being separate from every named company.

**Firewall checks:** No confidential program contents; no later-era outcomes beyond the corporate guide's own "possibility" language.
