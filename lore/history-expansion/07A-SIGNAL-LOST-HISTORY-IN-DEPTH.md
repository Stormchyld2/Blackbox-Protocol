# Signal Lost: The Long Unravelling, 2031–2045
## An in-depth public history of the Signal Lost era

**Setting:** Blackbox: Signal Lost
**Status:** Expanded draft. This document builds on `07-SIGNAL-LOST-HISTORY.md`, which it supplements and does not replace. Approved dates and story direction are used as written. Everything marked **(proposed)** is new in this draft and stays editable until it is reconciled with the era chronology, the county atlas and the GM Secret History.
**Fiction notice:** All named people, incidents, companies, settlements and counties in this document are fictional unless they are named as real geographic reference points. No real person, community, church, county, agency or military installation is implied to have taken part in any fictional wrongdoing.
**Rules notice:** This is setting history only. It does not add, change or imply mechanics. Where it mentions service levels, learning, rationing or settlement change, the authoritative rules in F02, F19–F24 and the Transition Handbook govern play.

## How to use this history

### What this document is

Signal Lost covers the fifteen years between the end of the contemporary Core era and the beginning of Dead Zone. The approved metadata fixes those boundaries: **Signal Lost begins in 2031** and **Dead Zone begins in 2046**. Everything in between is a long transition, not a single catastrophe. This history describes how ordinary institutions bent, failed, were repaired, failed again and were replaced across those years, using the default campaign trajectory.

It is written in the voice of a public historian working from records that survive into the 2040s: council minutes, ration ledgers, radio logs, school exercise books, court transcripts from emergency tribunals, oral testimony, company bulletins and the wreckage of the old internet. That voice matters. Players in a Signal Lost campaign live inside this history and can only know what someone recorded, remembered or rumoured. The historian says so when the record is thin.

### What this document is not

- **It is not a destiny.** Campaigns that begin in Core can continue indefinitely without collapse (F02). Collapse can be regional, national or global, temporary or sustained. A GM who runs a linked campaign from Core may change everything below.
- **It is not one cause.** Canon deliberately does not fix a single global trigger for Signal Lost, and this document does not invent one. See *The Cause Question* below.
- **It is not a conspiracy narrative.** The Blackbox legacy left infrastructure, procedures and archives behind, but no single program, corporation, government or hidden group dictates what happens in this era. The setting remains morally neutral: every institution in it contains decent people and corrupt ones.
- **It is not a spoiler for Core.** Nothing here belongs in the Core Player's Handbook. Core players receive events knowable by January 2026 (publication firewall, register 09).

### Era knowledge

| Who | What they can know |
|---|---|
| A Core-era player | Nothing in this document. |
| A Signal Lost player, native or transitional | Public events up to the campaign's current date, filtered through local sources. Distant regions arrive as rumour, radio fragments or traveller accounts. |
| A Dead Zone player | The broad public shape of this era, as remembered and argued about. Official histories, especially in Heritage territory, may be distorted. |
| The GM | This document plus the GM Secret History. The GM decides which events are official in their campaign. |

## The Cause Question

Ask ten survivors in 2046 why the world changed and you will get ten answers. Some blame a particular year's storms. Some blame the grid. Some blame debt, war overseas, a lost harvest, a disease, the old Blackbox procedures, corporations, governments, foreigners or God. Most of them are describing something true about their own region, and none of them is describing the whole.

**Canon position.** No single global trigger is established. The era is defined by its *pattern*, which is approved: failures in supply, payment, communication and institutional coordination occur **unevenly**, become **persistent** rather than temporary, and are met by repair, migration, mutual aid and negotiation **before** people abandon established institutions.

**For the GM.** F02's canon principles let the GM select official events. A campaign can name its own catalyst, or several, from the kinds of pressure the Core era already contains: infrastructure deterioration, contested supply chains, financial and payment failures, severe weather, conflict abroad, public health emergencies, cyber or control-system failures, or classified legacy systems behaving in ways their custodians no longer understand. The Core GM Handbook may close with uncertain warning signs, but they point in several directions on purpose. Whatever is chosen, record it in the campaign history, because the campaign's history is authoritative for that campaign.

**For the historian's voice.** This document refers to the era's opening as *the strain*, *the failures* and, later, *the Unravelling*, the name most survivors use. It never claims to know the full cause, because no one in the setting does.

## Era overview

The Signal Lost era unfolds in six overlapping phases. The dates are the default trajectory; regions move through them at different speeds, and some never reach the later ones.

| Phase | Years | Shape of the period |
|---|---|---|
| I. The Strain | 2031–2033 | Failures become frequent and then persistent. Institutions still function and still promise recovery. |
| II. The Patchwork | 2033–2035 | Services survive in islands. Money weakens. Communities begin making their own arrangements. |
| III. Competing Institutions | 2034–2037 | Utilities, councils, contractors, churches, cooperatives and private compounds compete to keep things running and to decide who gets what. |
| IV. Legitimacy Contests | 2037–2039 | The argument shifts from "who can fix it" to "who has the right to govern". |
| V. Routes and Reach | 2039–2042 | Roads, bridges, ports and passes become the arteries of power. Localised violence follows control of movement. |
| VI. Successor Authorities | 2043–2045 | New arrangements harden into the councils, cooperatives, guilds, enclaves and regimes that Dead Zone inherits. |

## Phase I — The Strain, 2031–2033

### What people noticed first

The earliest records of the era are not dramatic. They are complaints. A pharmacy cannot fill a prescription for the third week running. A card reader fails at a grocery store and the manager accepts handwritten IOUs from regulars. A school district cancels bus routes because a parts order has been "in transit" for two months. A county alert system sends a test message at three in the morning and then nothing during an actual flood. Each event has an explanation, and in 2031 people accept the explanation, because in the Core era things broke and were fixed all the time.

What changes in 2031 and 2032 is the **rate** and the **recovery time**. Outages that used to last hours last days. Shortages that used to clear in a week linger for a season. A failure in one system starts to cause failures in others: a fuel delay strands repair crews, which lengthens a power outage, which spoils refrigerated medicine, which overwhelms a clinic. The historian's term for this is *coupling*. Survivors simply call it the year everything started waiting on everything else.

### Institutions still believe in recovery

Through 2031 and most of 2032, governments, utilities and companies behave as though the disruption is temporary, because that is what all their plans assume. National and state governments issue emergency declarations, fund overtime, open distribution points and promise that normal service will return. International organisations coordinate aid between regions that are still stable and regions that are not. Many of these efforts work. The history of this phase is full of real successes: a regional hospital kept open by a mutual-aid agreement among three counties, a port that reopened after volunteer crane operators came out of retirement, a rural electric cooperative that restored power faster than the city utility because its linemen knew every pole.

The problem is not that institutions fail to act. It is that their plans were built for emergencies that end. By late 2032, emergency funds are spent, overtime crews are exhausted and the promised return to normal has been postponed so often that people stop planning around it.

### Money starts to mean different things

Payment systems are among the first things people stop trusting. The record shows a gradual slide rather than a crash:

- **2031:** Intermittent card and transfer outages. Cash demand spikes; some banks limit withdrawals. Online retailers, including Cindrix Market, begin quoting "estimated" rather than guaranteed delivery windows in affected regions and quietly drop some areas from same-day service.
- **2032:** Prices diverge sharply between regions and even between neighbouring towns. Store credit, tabs and local scrip appear. Employers in some districts pay partly in goods: fuel vouchers, food boxes, housing.
- **2033:** In the hardest-hit areas, national currency still exists but no longer reliably buys essentials. Barter and local accounting begin in earnest. Elsewhere, ordinary commerce continues with frequent interruptions.

Wealth does not vanish evenly, and the history of the era is partly a history of people discovering what their wealth was actually made of. A retirement account that cannot be accessed is worth little; a well, a woodlot, a working truck or a trade is worth a great deal.

### Communication thins

The era's name comes from this phase. Mobile networks degrade first in rural areas, where towers depend on long power and backhaul lines, and then in cities, where demand overwhelms damaged capacity. Broadcast radio outlasts the internet in most places. Amateur and GMRS operators, county emergency radio and ORG-03-style volunteer networks become the backbone of local communication. The phrase "signal lost", originally the error message on phones that could not connect, becomes slang for anything that has stopped working, then for anyone who has stopped answering, and finally for the era itself.

Information quality collapses faster than information volume. When national news becomes intermittent, rumours fill the gap. Records from 2032 and 2033 are full of confident reports that turned out to be false: cities that were "gone" and were not, aid convoys that never existed, outbreaks that were not outbreaks. Historians of the era treat any single-source account from these years with caution.

### Late 2033: the turn

Most regional histories mark late 2033 as the point where the strain became something else. The usual markers are:

- the first winter in which a region's grid, water or heating cannot be restored before the season turns;
- the first sustained evacuation from a town to its neighbours;
- the first time a local official publicly says that outside help is not coming soon.

Where these markers arrive, Phase II begins. Where they do not, communities may remain in a long, tense version of Phase I for years.

### Heritage Ranch becomes the Heritage Settlement in practice

In west-central Texas, the community still called **Heritage Ranch** by many neighbours has already been formally renamed. The Mercer Succession began around 2028, the siblings replaced the timber sign with **THE HERITAGE SETTLEMENT** gateway in 2029, and the 2030 membership code introduced mandatory service, internal hearings and tighter controls on leaving (approved story direction; exact mechanism of the succession pending).

When the strain arrives, the Settlement is unusually well placed. The original 135-acre cattle ranch, bought by Elias Mercer in 2008 expressly to build a survivalist compound, has a working herd, gardens, food storage, workshops, fuel reserves, trained members and a disciplined internal structure. In 2031 and 2032, Nathan Mercer's transport crews deliver food and water to neighbours during outages, and Rebecca Mercer's family programmes run emergency childcare and schooling. Many neighbours are grateful, and the gratitude is genuine.

The record also shows the beginning of what the historian calls **protected dependency**. Help is offered freely the first time. The second time, it comes with a request: a few hours of labour, a share of a harvest, permission for Settlement vehicles to cross a property. By 2033, some agreements require recognition of Settlement adjudication in disputes. A neighbour who declines is rarely threatened; they simply stop receiving help. Nathan and Rebecca present every restriction as necessary protection in an emergency. Elias, now in his late sixties and no longer in practical control, objects in private letters that survive into the Dead Zone era.

**The South Granary Dispute (proposed, 2033).** In the autumn of 2033, a quarrel inside the Settlement council over whether grain reserved for members may be distributed to unaffiliated families becomes public. A relief convoy leaves the South Granary bound for displaced families on the county road. What happened to it is disputed: the official account says it was turned back for its own safety after reports of raiders; two drivers later said they were ordered to return because the recipients had refused membership terms; a third account says part of the load was delivered and part was diverted. Jonah Price's quartermaster ledger for that month, if it can be found, would settle at least the quantities. It is the first of many Heritage incidents whose truth depends on which records survive.

## Phase II — The Patchwork, 2033–2035

### Islands of service

By 2034, the map of working services looks less like a national network and more like an archipelago. A region's fortunes depend on a few concrete questions: Does it have local power generation? Clean water that does not depend on distant pumping? Food grown within a day's travel? People who know how to maintain what it has? Institutions that people trust?

Places that can answer yes to most of those questions remain recognisable. Places that cannot begin to empty. Cities do not uniformly fail; some districts with strong neighbourhood organisations and local utilities outlast suburbs that depended entirely on cars and deliveries. Rural areas are not uniformly safe; farms that relied on imported fuel, fertiliser and feed suffer badly, while those with draft animals, seed stock and diversified crops adapt.

### Calder County (Region 1)

The Calder County records are among the best preserved of the era, because the county emergency operations centre in **Fenwick** kept paper logs after its servers failed.

- **Fenwick** swells with evacuees from outer towns during the winters of 2033 and 2034. The regional hospital drops to generator power; for long stretches, fuel is the most important line in the county ledger. ORG-02 escorts fuel convoys from the rail yard, and the price of those escorts becomes a running argument in council meetings. The county council keeps meeting, but its authority shrinks as staff stop showing up and its reserves run out.
- **Alder Crossing** becomes the county's food hub. Its grain elevator, feed store and farm families feed a population that temporarily doubles as people leave Fenwick. Storage and sanitation strain badly. The town's volunteer fire company turns into its de facto government for a year, because it is the only organisation everyone trusts.
- **ORG-03**, the county volunteer corps, runs shelters, first aid and radio. Its radio net, operating from the hilltop alert site long after the automated system failed, carries most of the county's news through 2035.
- **ORG-01**, the infrastructure contractor (optionally CindrixOps), keeps its crews working on the county grid and water plant well past the point where its contracts can be paid. Its regional manager later testified that crews stayed because their own families depended on the systems. Some crews leave for better-supplied counties; the ones who stay become some of the most valuable people in the region.

### Aldane Coast (Region 2)

**Port Aldane** was built on imports, and the history of its decline is a history of ships that stopped coming. In 2033 and 2034 container traffic falls to a trickle. Shelters fill, many residents leave inland, and the port itself becomes a contested resource: whoever controls the cranes, the fuel and the harbour radio controls what little trade remains. The university campus becomes an unlikely centre of practical knowledge; its engineering, agriculture and medical staff run classes for anyone who will come.

**Kestrel Bay**, a fishing village of about nine hundred, is cut off from supply early. It survives on its catch and its gardens. Its boat owners, who had been feuding for a generation, are forced into a sharing arrangement that becomes one of the era's small success stories. The arrival of the first refugee boat in 2034 (proposed) forces the village to decide how many newcomers it can feed. The answer it gives, and the way it enforces that answer, is remembered differently by villagers and by the people turned away.

### Ridgeback Highlands (Region 3)

**Hollis Gap** depends on one paved pass and one small hydroelectric dam. When the pass is blocked by snow or damage, the town is alone; when the dam works, it has light, heat and workshop power that much of the lowlands lacks. Through 2034 and 2035 the dam's maintenance crew, a handful of people with the right skills, becomes the most important institution in the highlands. A trapped convoy in the winter of 2034 (proposed) forces Hollis Gap to choose between sharing its limited heating wood with strangers and protecting its own households.

### Heritage: Elias Mercer's death (working date 2034)

Elias Mercer dies in 2034, at about seventy (working date; 2033–2035 permitted if the era chronology requires). His death is a public event in the county, and the way it is remembered is one of the era's sharpest historical disputes.

Rebecca's memorial addresses present her father as the visionary author of a disciplined new order and quote his sermons on work, faith and preparation. They omit his insistence on voluntary membership. Nathan invokes his father's logistical competence and his duty to defend the community. A minority of original members, among them former school administrator **Mara Ellis**, preserve a different account: that Elias spent his last years objecting to the direction of the Settlement, and that his letters say so. The original **HERITAGE RANCH** sign, by later oral accounts, was kept in private storage. Its whereabouts are disputed.

### Everyday life in the patchwork

For most people, the Patchwork years are not about violence. They are about time. Hours once spent commuting are now spent fetching water, queuing for rations, repairing things, tending gardens, splitting wood and walking. Social life contracts to the distance a person can walk or cycle. Neighbourhoods reorganise themselves around whoever has a skill: the retired nurse, the mechanic, the man with the chickens, the woman with the ham radio licence.

Schools close and then reopen in church halls, libraries and living rooms. Children of the era learn arithmetic from ration ledgers and geography from refugee accounts. Funerals become community labour. Weddings become trade fairs. People keep paper diaries again, and those diaries become some of the richest sources of the period.

Transitional characters from the Core era feel this phase most sharply. A detective still knows how to investigate, but investigation now runs on interviews, paper records and footwork. A software engineer still understands systems, but may have no working systems to understand. Competencies the era demands, such as food preservation, livestock care, radio operation and off-grid repair, must be learned through practice, mentorship and failure. The Transition Handbook owns that procedure.

## Phase III — Competing Institutions, 2034–2037

### Who keeps the lights on

The defining question of these years is practical: when the old institutions can no longer provide a service, who does? The answer varies from town to town, and the competition between possible answers shapes the next decade.

- **Municipal and county governments** retain legal authority and public buildings but lose staff and money. Where they adapt, by recruiting volunteers, pooling resources with neighbours and holding open meetings, they often survive into Dead Zone as settlement councils. Where they do not, they become empty offices.
- **Utilities and contractors** like ORG-01 hold the knowledge and equipment to run grids, water plants and radio relays. Their leverage grows as their clients' ability to pay shrinks. Some become, in effect, local governments; some hand their equipment to councils; a few sell their services to the highest bidder.
- **Private security and logistics firms** like ORG-02 control trucks, warehouses and armed guards. They are indispensable for moving fuel and food, and their price, whether paid in money, goods or political influence, is a constant source of conflict.
- **Hospitals and clinics** become centres of community life and of moral dispute. Who is treated first? Can care be conditioned on anything?
- **Churches, mosques, temples and civic groups** run food banks, shelters and schools. Some remain open to all; some begin to prefer their own members.
- **Cooperatives** (TPL-B) form around shared fields, tools and labour registries. They thrive in farm country.
- **Private compounds** like the Heritage Settlement offer real services in exchange for obligations.

The era's first community successes are not necessarily the best armed. The historian's consistent finding is that maintenance skills and **trusted distribution** matter more than weapons. A council that can show its ration ledger to anyone who asks keeps the peace longer than a militia that cannot.

### Cindrix and the end of the delivery age

Cindrix, the retail corporation whose slogan was *Everything. Within reach.*, is a useful lens for the end of Core-era commerce. Cindrix is not a villain in this history, and nothing below claims otherwise. Through 2032 and 2033, Cindrix Market's regional stock thins, delivery estimates lengthen and service areas contract. By 2035, many regions see no deliveries at all. Cindrix warehouses, some still stocked when their supply lines failed, become important local sites: some are opened by staff to their communities, some are guarded by ORG-02-style contractors, some are claimed by councils and some are looted. Abandoned shipments in rail yards and on highway shoulders become a recurring feature of Signal Lost adventures. (The Cindrix Market rules remain a separate workstream; no service-degradation mechanics are established here.)

### The Blackbox legacy in the open

The Blackbox Initiative, activated in 1991 and later associated with the designation Blackbox Protocol, was built to protect essential continuity during geopolitical emergencies. In the Signal Lost era, its legacy surfaces in fragments: hardened communication sites that still work when nothing else does, emergency stockpiles whose inventories no one can fully reconcile, archived procedures that local officials discover in safes and server rooms, and personnel trained under old continuity plans who now find themselves in charge of a pumping station or a relay tower.

Public knowledge of these fragments grows during Phase III, because people stumble across them. Public understanding does not grow nearly as fast. A 2017 classified audit had already concluded that no one could reliably inventory the whole legacy, and in Signal Lost that is felt on the ground: a council finds a sealed facility it has no records for; a radio operator picks up a scheduled transmission in an old format; a former contractor claims to know where a stockpile is. **The legacy is infrastructure and archives, not a hidden government.** It does not dictate the era's political future, and treating it as an omnipotent explanation is one of the era's most common and most dangerous rumours. GM-only details belong in the GM Secret History.

### Heritage: from refuge to ring

From 2035, the Heritage Settlement's influence extends beyond its original boundaries. The historian identifies four mechanisms, each documented in surviving records:

1. **Debt.** Neighbouring families who received food, fuel or repairs owe labour or land.
2. **Abandoned land.** Properties left by evacuated families are occupied "for safekeeping" by Settlement members.
3. **Negotiated transfers.** Some landowners sell or lease to the Settlement willingly, in exchange for protection and supply.
4. **Security arrangements.** Nathan builds a chain of controlled depots and patrol districts along the county roads.

Rebecca builds the administrative side: settlement registries, schooling standards, approved religious observances and a hierarchy of local **stewards** who administer affiliated properties. Independent families negotiate exceptions, sometimes successfully. **Jonah Price**, the Settlement's quartermaster, keeps ledgers that later show food distribution in these years was both genuinely effective and politically selective.

**Dr. Celia Rourke**, a rural physician serving several communities, publicly refuses to condition medical care on political affiliation. Her clinic, which treats Settlement members and outsiders alike, becomes a neutral meeting place and an irritant to Settlement leadership.

### Elsewhere in the world

This document's regional detail is concentrated on the United States because that is where the setting's initial coverage lies, but the setting is global and the Signal Lost era is not an American story with foreign scenery.

- **Europe:** Dense rail networks, cross-border energy grids and strong municipal traditions shape a patchwork of responses. Some countries coordinate effectively across borders; others turn inward. Local energy and transport become the measure of a region's resilience.
- **East and Southeast Asia:** Maritime commerce and industrial supply chains are hit hard when trade falters, but many communities have deep experience of typhoons, floods and earthquakes and the local institutions to match. In the Philippines and elsewhere, barangay-level organisation and local disaster networks prove more durable than national logistics.
- **South Asia:** Urban density, monsoon agriculture, water systems and regional trade produce several distinct trajectories. Local food systems and community water management matter more than imports.
- **Africa:** There is no continental collapse narrative. National and local histories differ enormously. Local engineers, cooperatives, merchants and civic groups keep services running in many places, and some regions that were less dependent on fragile global supply chains weather the era better than wealthier ones.
- **Latin America and the Caribbean:** Distinct port, agricultural, energy and municipal networks face different pressures. Islands depend on shipping and local food; inland agricultural regions on markets and roads.
- **Oceania:** Island logistics, maritime travel and local food systems become critical. Small island communities with traditional fishing and gardening knowledge adapt in ways that import-dependent towns cannot.

In every region, people try repair, migration, mutual aid and negotiation before abandoning established institutions. National governments and international bodies continue to exist and act throughout the era, with varying reach.

## Phase IV — Legitimacy Contests, 2037–2039

### From "who can fix it" to "who has the right"

By 2037, most surviving communities have some arrangement for food, water, shelter and dispute resolution. The question shifts from whether those things exist to who controls them and by what right. The historian finds four recurring arguments in the records of these years:

1. **Who may levy obligations?** Can a council require labour, a share of a harvest or service on a watch rota? Can a cooperative? A company? A compound?
2. **Who adjudicates disputes?** Surviving courts, emergency tribunals, church elders, cooperative councils and private hearings all compete to settle conflicts over land, theft, debt and violence.
3. **Who protects the roads?** Escorts, checkpoints and patrols bring safety and tolls in equal measure.
4. **Who controls shared stores?** Granaries, fuel depots, medicine stocks and seed banks are the most valuable property in any district.

Emergency councils follow two broad paths. Some become **democratic local governments**, holding elections, publishing ledgers and rotating offices. Others harden into **patronage networks**, in which access to food, work and protection depends on personal loyalty to a leader or family. Many communities move back and forth between the two, and the struggle between them is one of the era's richest sources of play.

### The First Steward Walkout (proposed, 2037)

In 2037, several of the Heritage Settlement's locally appointed stewards resign together over Rebecca Mercer's education and membership requirements. Their stated objections are that new curricula teach loyalty rather than skills, that membership status has become a condition for rations, and that internal hearings no longer allow residents to speak for themselves.

The walkout matters for two reasons. First, the former stewards, experienced administrators who know how the Settlement's systems work, form a small network of **independent communities** that favour local self-governance. Second, it is the first time a group from inside the Settlement's own structure publicly contradicts its account of itself. Settlement histories later call the walkout a betrayal; the independents call it a stand. Mara Ellis, who documented the curriculum changes, is associated with the walkout in later testimony, though her exact role is disputed.

### Rival accounts and missing records

Among displaced families across the county, rival accounts of the Settlement's food and security agreements circulate. Some records are incomplete, some were deliberately altered and some were simply lost to fire, flood or neglect. A ration agreement signed in 2035 may exist in three versions: the Settlement's file copy, the family's copy and the version a steward remembers. Investigation in Signal Lost often turns on exactly this kind of problem, and F15's distinction between observation, inference, rumour, misinformation and verified fact applies with full force.

### Calder, Aldane and Ridgeback in the legitimacy years (proposed examples)

- In **Fenwick**, the old county council and an ORG-03-led emergency committee hold competing meetings for most of 2038. The dispute is settled not by force but by a joint ledger audit, after which the two bodies merge. The merged council is the ancestor of the Dead Zone town council.
- At **Port Aldane**, boat owners, dockworkers and the remaining ORG-02 port security contract fight over harbour access. A harbour board forms that gives fishing crews a vote. Over time it becomes the trade guild (TPL-C) that controls boats in the Dead Zone era.
- In **Hollis Gap**, the dam crew refuses to accept orders from a lowland authority that cannot guarantee their families' food. They negotiate directly with the highland farms instead, a decision that gives the town real leverage for decades.

### The Briar Hollow question

The approved Core starting town of **Briar Hollow, West Virginia** has no established Signal Lost history, and this document does not give it one. A linked campaign that began in Briar Hollow should write the town's Signal Lost record from what the players did in Core. The town and its unnamed county are well suited to the patterns above: a small community with local institutions, contested county authority and a strong memory of earlier hard times.

## Phase V — Routes and Reach, 2039–2042

### Movement becomes power

As local arrangements stabilise, the arteries between them become the focus of conflict. Roads, bridges, rail lines, ports and mountain passes carry the trade that every community now depends on. Whoever controls them can tax, protect, delay or deny. The historian describes this period as the moment power stopped being about what a community had and started being about where it sat.

Escorted shipments, checkpoint tolls, raids and reprisals become a regular part of life along major routes. Bandits who prey on travellers exist; so do armed groups (TPL-D) that protect travellers for a fee, and the difference between them is often a matter of perspective. Trade guilds and merchant houses (TPL-C) emerge to organise convoys, negotiate passage and keep credit records. Pack animals and carts return to roads that once carried freight trucks.

### The County Road Conflicts (proposed, 2039–2042)

The most consequential events in the Heritage region are the **County Road Conflicts**, a series of fictional disputes across the fictional Texas county around the Settlement. They are not one war. They are many incidents with independent causes, and they should be written that way. The record includes:

- **Bridge disputes:** contests over who may cross key river and creek bridges, who maintains them and who may close them.
- **Grain shipments:** escorted convoys between Settlement depots and affiliated farms, and the communities along the route that resent or rely on them.
- **Road access:** Settlement checkpoints that begin as safety measures and become permit systems.
- **Armed escorts:** competition between Settlement escorts, independent guards and opportunistic raiders.
- **Raids and retaliation:** attacks on travellers and depots, followed by reprisals that sometimes fall on people who had nothing to do with the original raid.

Neither side is uniformly blameless. Some rival groups genuinely prey on travellers. Settlement officers increasingly treat dissent as hostile action. **Silas Trent**, Nathan's district security commander, earns both praise for keeping routes open and condemnation for collective punishments. The conflicts harden Nathan's command structure into something closer to a standing force and give Rebecca a justification for **emergency courts**.

By 2042, the Settlement's patrol districts cover most of the county's road network. Its borders are still contested and some independent communities, including several founded by the First Steward walkout, still hold out. Dead Zone residents remember the Road Conflicts as **the turning point from negotiated protection to enforced territorial rule**.

### Regional examples along the routes

These are illustrations, not a single worldwide script:

- **Coastal communities** depend on boat repair, fishing and small coastal trade. Harbour control matters more than roads.
- **Highland settlements** depend on passes, timber and small hydroelectric plants. A town like Hollis Gap that keeps its dam running can trade power and workshop time for food.
- **Inland towns** depend on grain stores and clinics. Alder Crossing's grain elevator, already the heart of its community, becomes the centre of a regional seed and tool exchange.
- **Rail corridors** become salvage sites and, in a few places, are restored for hand-car and animal-drawn freight.

### Life on the road

A traveller in 2040 carries papers. Depending on the region, these may be council passes, guild convoy tickets, Settlement permits, letters of introduction from a respected clinic or church, or nothing at all, in which case the traveller depends on reputation and luck. Inns reappear at crossroads, often in old motels and truck stops. News travels with merchants and radio operators. The era's oral culture takes shape here: road songs, warnings about particular bridges, stories about honest and dishonest guards.

## Phase VI — Successor Authorities, 2043–2045

### The world Dead Zone inherits

The last years of the era are not a final collapse. They are a hardening. The arrangements improvised over the previous decade become institutions with names, rules and histories of their own. By 2045, a traveller crossing a region might pass through:

- **surviving elected councils** that trace their authority to pre-era governments;
- **cooperatives** that govern farm districts through rotating councils and labour registries;
- **trade associations and guilds** that control ports, convoy routes and markets;
- **corporate enclaves** where a former contractor or company runs services for those it employs;
- **religious and civic communities** that govern their own members;
- **opportunistic armed regimes** that rule by force;
- **abandoned and contested zones** where no one holds authority.

The old Blackbox legacy leaves infrastructure and archives behind, and some successor authorities use them, but **no one program dictates the world's political future**. National governments still exist in name and sometimes in practice, especially in regions that weathered the era well.

### The Settlement Compact (proposed, 2043–2045)

In the Heritage region, the Settlement leadership imposes a common system of obligations on subordinate villages and farms. The **Settlement Compact** guarantees specified food allocations, basic defence and access to repair services. In exchange it requires production quotas, labour and service obligations, travel controls and loyalty to the governing council.

The Compact's terms are unequal and its enforcement is often coercive. It is also, for many households, the most reliable source of food and protection they have known in a decade. Some communities sign willingly, some sign under pressure and some refuse and live with the consequences. Surviving copies differ, and the question of what the Compact actually promised becomes a political issue in the Dead Zone era.

By 2045, the Heritage Settlement has become a **territorial regime**, though its borders remain contested. Nathan Mercer, about sixty, commands its security; Rebecca Mercer, about fifty-six, governs its doctrine, schools, membership and tribunals. The original 135-acre ranch remains its capital and the seat of the Mercer family. The regime is not confined to that parcel, and its authority does not extend across Texas. It is not a secret arm of the Blackbox Initiative, Cindrix, ORG-01 or any real church.

### Calder, Aldane and Ridgeback at the threshold (example states)

- **Fenwick** is held by a council built from ORG-03 volunteers and surviving officials. Its population is a fraction of what it was. The hospital has shrunk to a clinic with a few trained staff, and the rail yard is being salvaged for metal.
- **Alder Crossing** is a farming cooperative centred on its grain elevator, trading seed and tools across the region.
- **Port Aldane** has a few thousand people around the harbour and the university farm. Its boats are controlled by a trade guild.
- **Kestrel Bay** is a self-reliant fishing community with salting and drying sheds, trading fish for grain and tools.
- **Hollis Gap** still has power from its dam, a skilled maintenance crew and the leverage that comes with them.

These are one possible path. Population and service changes are never automatic (F22); the GM records the events that cause them.

## Living through Signal Lost

### Generations

The era is experienced very differently by age.

- **People who were adults in the Core era** carry the memory of a world that worked. Many of them spend the era trying to restore it; some spend it mourning; some reinvent themselves entirely. Their skills from before are real but often mismatched to what the era demands.
- **People who were children in the Core era** come of age during the Unravelling. They are often the most adaptable, learning both the old world's knowledge from their parents and the new world's skills from necessity.
- **Children born during Signal Lost** know nothing else. By 2046 the oldest of them are fifteen. They are the first native generation of the Dead Zone era.

### Knowledge and learning

The era rediscovers apprenticeship. Manuals, textbooks and repair guides become treasured property. Libraries that survive become community institutions of real importance, and librarians who keep them open are remembered as heroes. Mentors who know how to preserve food, deliver babies, repair engines, keep bees or run a radio station are sought after and sometimes fought over. The setting's learning rules (progressive learning, transferable experience, real competency deficits) reflect this history directly.

### Faith, meaning and argument

People argue about what the era means. Some see judgment, some see a test, some see the natural result of fragile systems, and some refuse to see meaning at all. Religious communities grow and split. New movements appear, most of them small and local. The setting remains neutral: faith is a source of both great charity and great control in this era, as it is in every other.

### Hope

The historian's last observation about the era is the one most often forgotten in Dead Zone: Signal Lost is also a history of people keeping each other alive. Mutual aid is the most common response to disaster in the record, not violence. Councils that publish their ledgers, clinics that treat everyone, crews that keep the water running without pay, villages that share their catch and teachers who hold class in a basement by lamplight are as much a part of this history as checkpoints and raids. Reconstruction is possible. The era's ending is not written in stone, and the Dead Zone that follows has room for recovery as well as for regimes.

## Primary sources and artefacts

A Signal Lost or Dead Zone campaign can use the era's surviving records as evidence trails. Common types include:

| Artefact | What it can reveal |
|---|---|
| Council minutes and emergency logs | Who decided what, when, and who objected. |
| Ration and quartermaster ledgers | Who received food, how much, and who was excluded. Jonah Price's ledgers are the best-known example. |
| Radio logs and recordings | Distant events, timings of failures, messages from people later lost. |
| Ration agreements and the Settlement Compact | Terms that different parties remember differently. |
| School exercise books and curricula | What children were taught, and how that changed. Mara Ellis's records document Heritage's changes. |
| Elias Mercer's letters and sermons | His objections to the Settlement's direction, contradicting official histories. |
| The original HERITAGE RANCH sign | Physical evidence that the institution once understood itself differently. Whereabouts disputed. |
| Road permits and convoy tickets | Who could travel where, and under whose authority. |
| Maintenance notebooks | How a grid, dam or water plant was kept running, and by whom. |
| Personal diaries and oral testimony | Everyday life, and accounts that official records omit. |
| Blackbox-era documents | Fragments of continuity planning found in sealed facilities. Treat with care; see GM Secret History. |
