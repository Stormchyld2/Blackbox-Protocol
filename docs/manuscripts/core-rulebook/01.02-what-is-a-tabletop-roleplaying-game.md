# 1.2 — What Is a Tabletop Roleplaying Game?

A tabletop roleplaying game, commonly abbreviated as TTRPG, is a form of collaborative storytelling in which participants assume the roles of fictional characters and explore a shared world. Unlike a traditional board game, the experience is not confined to a predetermined set of moves, a fixed playing area, or a single condition for victory.

Instead, the story develops through conversation, imagination, decisions, and the application of rules that establish how characters interact with their surroundings.

Each participant contributes to the experience. The players determine the actions of their characters, while the Game Master describes the world, portrays its inhabitants, and adjudicates the consequences of those actions. Together, they create a story whose outcome is not entirely known to anyone when play begins.

## The Conversation at the Table

At its most fundamental level, a tabletop roleplaying game is a conversation.

The Game Master describes a situation, providing the players with information their characters would reasonably possess. The players consider that information, ask questions, discuss their options, and decide how their characters respond.

The Game Master then determines what happens as a result.

Imagine that your character arrives at a municipal records office to examine documents relating to a property dispute. The Game Master describes the building, the reception area, and the clerk responsible for processing requests.

You might approach the clerk and explain why you need the documents. You might examine the publicly available records, contact someone familiar with the property, or arrange to return when a particular employee is available.

You are not selecting from a predetermined menu of actions. You are deciding what a person in your character's circumstances might reasonably attempt.

The Game Master considers your decision in relation to the situation, the capabilities of your character, and the circumstances surrounding the action. Where the outcome is uncertain and meaningful, the rules may be used to determine the result.

That result becomes part of the developing story, providing new information, changing circumstances, or presenting another decision.

This exchange forms the foundation of tabletop roleplaying.

## Playing a Character

During play, you portray an individual whose experiences, abilities, beliefs, and circumstances may differ considerably from your own.

Your character is represented mechanically by a character sheet. This record describes their attributes, skills, competencies, possessions, and other information relevant to the game. These details help establish what your character knows, what they are capable of accomplishing, and how they may respond to particular circumstances.

However, a character is more than the information recorded on a sheet.

They may have personal ambitions, professional responsibilities, friendships, family commitments, fears, convictions, and opinions about the world around them. Some of these characteristics may become important during a campaign, while others simply help you understand the person you are portraying.

Roleplaying does not require theatrical ability, an adopted accent, or extensive knowledge of your character's profession.

Some players prefer to speak directly as their characters:

“I would like to examine the original records before discussing the findings with anyone else.”

Others prefer to describe their character's actions:

“My character asks the clerk whether the original records are still available.”

Both approaches are equally valid. You may alternate between them naturally throughout a session.

The purpose of roleplaying is to make decisions from your character's perspective, not to demonstrate your ability as a performer.

## The Purpose of Rules

Without an established framework, disagreements could arise over what characters are capable of accomplishing and how uncertain situations should be resolved.

The rules provide that framework.

They establish a consistent relationship between a character's abilities, the difficulty of an action, and the circumstances under which it is attempted. They also provide a means of resolving situations in which success cannot reasonably be determined through description alone.

Not every action requires a dice roll.

A character walking through an unobstructed doorway, reading an ordinary newspaper, or preparing a familiar meal under normal circumstances will generally accomplish the task without mechanical resolution.

However, examining incomplete financial records under a strict deadline, repairing damaged equipment with limited resources, or persuading an uncooperative witness to provide information may involve uncertainty significant enough to require a check.

Dice introduce an element of uncertainty without removing the importance of character development or player judgment. A character's training and experience influence their chances of success, while preparation and surrounding circumstances may affect the difficulty of the task.

The rules exist to resolve uncertainty, not to replace conversation or imagination.

## The Shared World

The environment in which a roleplaying game takes place is collectively experienced, even though the Game Master is primarily responsible for describing it.

A location described at the table becomes a place the characters can visit. A conversation with another individual may establish a relationship that continues throughout the campaign. A decision made during one session may influence events several sessions later.

As play continues, the group develops a shared history.

Characters remember previous encounters, accumulate possessions, establish reputations, and experience the consequences of their decisions. Locations may change, organizations may respond to events, and individuals encountered earlier may return under different circumstances.

In Blackbox Protocol, these developments are not necessarily limited to events directly involving the player characters. The wider world continues to change, creating opportunities and difficulties that may emerge over time.

This continuity gives the campaign a sense of history and allows even seemingly ordinary decisions to acquire significance.

## Sessions, Adventures, and Campaigns

A tabletop roleplaying game may be played as a single experience or as an ongoing series of connected stories.

A **session** is an individual gathering during which the group plays. A session might cover several minutes of intense activity within the fictional world, an entire working day, or a much longer period summarized through narration.

An **adventure** is a sequence of related circumstances, challenges, and events. It may involve investigating a crime, completing a professional assignment, responding to an emergency, or pursuing an objective established by the characters.

An adventure designed to be completed in a single session is commonly called a **one-shot**.

A **campaign** is an extended series of sessions, often involving recurring characters, locations, relationships, and continuing developments. Campaigns may follow a central storyline, consist of loosely connected adventures, or develop primarily through the interests and decisions of the players.

There is no required length for a campaign. Some conclude after a handful of sessions, while others continue for months or years.

## Cooperation Rather Than Competition

Tabletop roleplaying is primarily a cooperative activity.

Although characters may disagree, compete for resources, or pursue conflicting objectives within the fictional world, the participants share a responsibility for creating an enjoyable experience.

The Game Master is not an opponent attempting to defeat the players. Their responsibility is to present the world, establish its challenges, and adjudicate events fairly.

Similarly, the players are not attempting to defeat the Game Master. They are participating in a shared experience through the actions of their characters.

This distinction is important. An opposing character may deceive, threaten, or obstruct the player characters without the Game Master personally opposing the players. A difficult situation may have unfavorable consequences without representing a failure of the group itself.

The participants are collaborators, even when the people they portray are not.

## What Does It Mean to Succeed?

Unlike many traditional games, tabletop roleplaying does not necessarily have a single winner.

Success may be measured by accomplishing an objective, resolving a mystery, protecting another person, developing a character, overcoming adversity, or reaching a satisfying conclusion to a story.

A campaign may contain setbacks, losses, difficult compromises, and objectives that remain unfinished. These events can contribute as much to a memorable experience as moments of triumph.

The success of the game itself is measured differently.

If the participants are engaged, their decisions matter, and the experience is one they value sharing, the game is accomplishing its purpose.

You are not required to tell a perfect story, make the most advantageous decision at every opportunity, or understand every rule before beginning.

You are here to participate in a world that develops through the imagination and decisions of everyone at the table.
