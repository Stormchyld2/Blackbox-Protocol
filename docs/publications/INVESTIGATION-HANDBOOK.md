## Working manuscript — Chapter 1: Constructing an investigation

**Status:** Substantive editorial draft, pending mechanical and semantic review. The Core Rulebook owns universal checks, social interaction, time and consequences. This handbook describes case design without creating competing rules.

An investigation begins with an event, not with a conclusion the Game Master expects players to guess. Write the private chronology first: what happened, who acted, who observed part of it, what records were created and what each person believes. A witness may lie to protect a colleague without knowing who caused the incident. A recording may be authentic yet misleading. Such distinctions let the players reconstruct events instead of finding a predetermined answer hidden behind one roll.

### Separate truth, evidence and knowledge

Keep three records. **Objective truth** is the GM-only account of events. **Available evidence** is the list of discoverable traces, testimony and records, including their provenance and limitations. **Character knowledge** is what the investigators have actually learned, including mistaken interpretations. Do not silently convert a player theory into a fact.

A document proves what its provenance and contents support, not everything someone might infer from it. A timestamp can establish when a device recorded an event without establishing who operated it. A fingerprint can establish contact without establishing when contact occurred. Describe observations separately from conclusions.

### Provide independent routes to essential discoveries

For each conclusion required to continue the case, prepare more than one reachable lead. Three witnesses repeating the same rumor are not three independent sources. A storage yard might be identified through a fuel receipt, a traffic recording and a separate witness who saw a vehicle arrive. Different approaches should permit progress when one clue is missed.

Use approved Core checks to determine detail, delay and consequences. A failed check should not erase the only route to an essential scene. Character expertise can supply technical context, recognize inconsistencies and suggest tests; it does not replace player reasoning. Conversely, players need not personally possess professional forensic knowledge to benefit from a competent character's observations.

### Case preparation worksheet

| Field | GM preparation |
|---|---|
| Case ID and era | Stable identifier; BP, SL or DZ |
| Initial report | Information available to players at the outset |
| Objective chronology | GM-only sequence and remaining uncertainties |
| Essential questions | What must be learned to proceed |
| Independent leads | Source, location, access and limitation for each |
| Conflicting accounts | Who disagrees, what they believe and why |
| Consequences of delay | What changes while investigators act |
| Resolution evidence | What would reasonably support the conclusion |
| Unresolved questions | Matters the case does not predetermine |

### People and institutions remain independent

Police, laboratories, private security, neighborhood organizations and public agencies have different responsibilities, access and incentives. Permission to inspect one organization's records does not imply access to every other institution. Give each participant a limited objective, a plausible reason to cooperate or refuse, and something they do not know. Disagreement does not automatically mean conspiracy.

In Signal Lost, disrupted communications can slow verification; in Dead Zone, missing archives may make local testimony more important. Neither circumstance makes all records worthless or all witnesses dishonest. Adapt access and consequences to the era without rewriting the underlying event.

### Worked case: The missing inspection log

**Player-facing opening:** A municipal water facility fails a routine quality check. Its most recent inspection log is missing, and two employees disagree about who last entered the records room.

**GM-only truth:** A supervisor removed the log after discovering that an overdue repair had been marked complete. The supervisor intended to avoid an immediate shutdown while arranging the repair. A technician independently noticed the same fault and reported it through another channel. Neither deliberately contaminated the water. The urgent problem is the unrepaired equipment.

**Independent leads:** The work-order record shows a discrepancy; the technician's dated report establishes prior notice; and an entry register places the supervisor in the archive after the failed check. Each supports a different part of the reconstruction. Access alone does not establish motive.

**Choices and consequences:** Investigators can prioritize public safety, preserve records, interview staff, seek independent testing or refer the matter to appropriate authorities. Delay may prolong the disruption. Premature accusations may damage cooperation. The facility can be repaired even if the disciplinary question remains unresolved; the case does not require a confession.

**Across eras:** BP may use electronic work orders and centralized laboratory records. SL may have delayed communications and interrupted testing. DZ may rely on surviving maintenance books and local witnesses. The evidentiary routes change, not the fundamental cause.

## Working manuscript — Chapter 2: Evidence, interviews and chain of custody

**Status:** Editorial draft. Mechanical authority: F15 Investigation and Evidence, F16 Social Interaction and Reputation, F11 time, and the Core resolution rules. No new modifiers, difficulty ladder, interrogation system or professional powers are introduced.

### 2.1 Discovering and recording evidence

An investigator first declares a question and a feasible method. The GM identifies what can be observed immediately, what requires access or equipment, and what remains uncertain. Obvious accessible evidence is described without a roll. Where a check is appropriate, Search addresses observation, Research addresses records, Forensics addresses interpretation, and PER addresses immediate cues, subject to the established competency rules. Resolve one lead with the existing check procedure; do not require multiple rolls merely because a clue has several descriptive details.

A failed check can cost time, yield less detail or leave a conclusion uncertain when those consequences have been established. It does not make an obvious clue disappear. The record should distinguish the original observation from the investigator's explanation of it. A damaged lock is an observation; the claim that a particular suspect forced it is an inference.

Use the following fields on each clue card:

| Field | Required information |
|---|---|
| Evidence ID | Stable case-local identifier |
| Source and location | Where the item or account originated |
| Observed / discovered | Event time if known; discovery time separately |
| Description | What was actually seen, heard or recovered |
| Classification | Observation, inference, rumor or verified |
| Reliability | Specific reason for confidence or uncertainty |
| Provenance | How the source or item can be traced |
| Custody | Current holder and recorded transfers |
| Links | Related evidence IDs and competing explanations |
| Access / visibility | Who may inspect it; player-facing or GM-only |

A verification label must be earned through corroboration or an appropriate method, not assigned because a player succeeds on a single roll. A clue can remain useful even when its source is imperfect.

### 2.2 Maintaining custody

When physical or digital evidence changes hands, record the transferring custodian, receiving custodian, time, condition and purpose. The record can be a simple note in Standard play, a custody log in Advanced play, or a more detailed procedural history in Simulation play. These are presentation depths of F15, not different rules for whether a clue is true.

A gap in custody creates a question about integrity. It does not automatically prove alteration, nor does it automatically invalidate everything the evidence suggests. The GM should ask what could realistically have changed during the gap and whether independent sources can resolve the uncertainty. A photograph of a damaged component may remain informative even if the original component was later misplaced; it is not a substitute for every test that could have been performed on the component itself.

### 2.3 Conducting interviews

Before an interview, prepare the person's knowledge, beliefs, interests, immediate concerns and authority. Keep these separate. Someone may have witnessed a delivery but misunderstood its purpose. A clerk may know where a file is stored yet lack permission to release it. An official may possess legal authority without having personal knowledge of the incident.

State what the character is trying to obtain: a factual account, cooperation, permission, a concession or a change of behavior. Routine cooperation needs no roll. An impossible concession does not become possible through a high result. For uncertain interactions use the Core's applicable social resolution and F16's boundaries. Success can secure a feasible declared outcome; it does not read minds, compel belief or certify that the speaker's account is true.

Repeated questioning without a changed approach or new leverage should not generate an endless series of rerolls. New documentary evidence, a changed relationship or a credible offer may materially alter the circumstances. Record what changed and why.

**Interview note:** Record the speaker, time, location, question, exact or summarized response, stated basis of knowledge, relevant pressures, corroborating or conflicting evidence, and what remains unasked. Label quotations clearly when wording matters.

### 2.4 Comparing competing explanations

Investigators should be able to hold multiple hypotheses without the GM forcing an immediate choice. A useful hypothesis sheet identifies what each explanation accounts for, what contradicts it and what observation would distinguish it from alternatives. Absence of evidence should not be treated as evidence of absence without considering whether the evidence could reasonably have survived or been found.

| Hypothesis | Supporting evidence | Contradictions / gaps | Next discriminating question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Records were deliberately concealed | Archive access after the incident | Access does not establish motive | Who benefited from the missing log? |
| Records were misfiled | Known filing problems | Does not explain a specific removal | Where were adjacent logs stored? |
| Records were destroyed accidentally | Reported water damage | No direct evidence this log was affected | Which materials show matching damage? |

A player may reach a reasonable conclusion before collecting every clue. The GM can adjudicate consequences from the actual evidence and established world state rather than requiring a predetermined number of clue cards.

### 2.5 Worked procedure: A contested inspection record

The investigators obtain a facility entry register showing that the supervisor entered the archive after the failed quality check. The entry is obvious to anyone with legitimate access to the register; no roll is needed merely to read the line. The timestamp establishes entry, not removal of the missing log.

A technician then provides a dated report describing the equipment fault. The technician is willing to discuss the fault but fears retaliation. The players can seek reassurance, ask about reporting channels or verify the report independently. A social check, if uncertainty warrants one, governs feasible cooperation rather than truthfulness itself.

Finally, a copy of the work order shows the repair marked complete. The investigators can compare its recorded completion date with the technician's observations. If the original log is recovered with an undocumented custody gap, that gap remains a reliability concern. The work order and technician's report still provide independent routes toward the safety problem.

**GM-only disposition:** The supervisor concealed the log to delay a shutdown, but the repair failure and the document removal are separate findings. Investigators can recommend immediate corrective action without proving every part of the supervisor's motive. An innocent employee's contradictory memory need not become deliberate deception.

### 2.6 Player and GM handout boundaries

Player handouts may contain discovered clue text, accessible documents, testimony and character-authored hypotheses. They must not include the objective chronology, undiscovered evidence, hidden motives or unrevealed reliability notes. GM case folders preserve both versions and label them distinctly. An offline digital export must enforce the same separation as printed handouts.
