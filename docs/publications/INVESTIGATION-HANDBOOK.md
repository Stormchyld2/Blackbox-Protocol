## Working manuscript — Chapter 1: Constructing an investigation

**Status:** Substantive editorial draft, pending mechanical and semantic review. The Core Rulebook owns universal checks, social interaction, time and consequences. This handbook describes case design without creating competing rules.

An investigation begins with an event, not with a conclusion the Game Master expects players to guess. Write the private chronology first: what happened, who acted, who observed part of it, what records were created and what each person believes. A witness may lie to protect a colleague without knowing who caused the incident. A recording may be authentic yet misleading. Such distinctions let the players reconstruct events instead of finding a predetermined answer hidden behind one roll.

### Separate truth, evidence and knowledge

Keep three records. **Objective truth** is the GM-only account of events. **Available evidence** is the list of discoverable traces, testimony and records, including their provenance and limitations. **Character knowledge** is what the investigators have actually learned, including mistaken interpretations. Do not silently convert a player theory into a fact.

A document proves what its provenance and contents support, not everything someone might infer from it. A timestamp can establish when a device recorded an event without establishing who operated it. A fingerprint can establish contact without establishing when contact occurred. Describe observations separately from conclusions.

### Provide independent routes to essential discoveries

For each conclusion required to continue the case, prepare more than one reachable lead. Three witnesses repeating the same rumor are not three independent sources. A storage yard might be identified through a fuel receipt, a traffic recording and a separate witness who saw a vehicle arrive. Different approaches should permit progress when one clue is missed.

Use approved Core checks to determine detail, delay and consequences. A failed check should not erase the only route to an essential scene. Character expertise can supply technical context, recognize inconsistencies and suggest tests; it does not replace player reasoning. Conversely, players need not personally possess professional forensic knowledge to benefit from a competent character's observations.

### Case preparation worksheet

| Field | GM preparation |
|---|---|
| Case ID and era | Stable identifier; BP, SL or DZ |
| Initial report | Information available to players at the outset |
| Objective chronology | GM-only sequence and remaining uncertainties |
| Essential questions | What must be learned to proceed |
| Independent leads | Source, location, access and limitation for each |
| Conflicting accounts | Who disagrees, what they believe and why |
| Consequences of delay | What changes while investigators act |
| Resolution evidence | What would reasonably support the conclusion |
| Unresolved questions | Matters the case does not predetermine |

### People and institutions remain independent

Police, laboratories, private security, neighborhood organizations and public agencies have different responsibilities, access and incentives. Permission to inspect one organization's records does not imply access to every other institution. Give each participant a limited objective, a plausible reason to cooperate or refuse, and something they do not know. Disagreement does not automatically mean conspiracy.

In Signal Lost, disrupted communications can slow verification; in Dead Zone, missing archives may make local testimony more important. Neither circumstance makes all records worthless or all witnesses dishonest. Adapt access and consequences to the era without rewriting the underlying event.

### Worked case: The missing inspection log

**Player-facing opening:** A municipal water facility fails a routine quality check. Its most recent inspection log is missing, and two employees disagree about who last entered the records room.

**GM-only truth:** A supervisor removed the log after discovering that an overdue repair had been marked complete. The supervisor intended to avoid an immediate shutdown while arranging the repair. A technician independently noticed the same fault and reported it through another channel. Neither deliberately contaminated the water. The urgent problem is the unrepaired equipment.

**Independent leads:** The work-order record shows a discrepancy; the technician's dated report establishes prior notice; and an entry register places the supervisor in the archive after the failed check. Each supports a different part of the reconstruction. Access alone does not establish motive.

**Choices and consequences:** Investigators can prioritize public safety, preserve records, interview staff, seek independent testing or refer the matter to appropriate authorities. Delay may prolong the disruption. Premature accusations may damage cooperation. The facility can be repaired even if the disciplinary question remains unresolved; the case does not require a confession.

**Across eras:** BP may use electronic work orders and centralized laboratory records. SL may have delayed communications and interrupted testing. DZ may rely on surviving maintenance books and local witnesses. The evidentiary routes change, not the fundamental cause.
